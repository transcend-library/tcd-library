Socially transitioning can be a fairly daunting task - and complicated. You might always have been known as ~~`deadname`~~ all your life, and changing it can take many, many reminders; if you choose not to change your name, it might take multiple reminders before people will begin referring to you with the pronouns you identify most strongly with.

These changes can also largely be legal in nature. You can change your name (and after a lengthy process that involves surgery, your gender marker) in Singapore as a Singaporean.

If you hold another nationality and am a permanent resident, and if your country recognises gender self-ID, changing your gender marker might be a simpler process.

Socially transitioning at work or in school however may also require dealing with administrative hassle - HR at work, for instance, or teachers in school. Some institutions may continue to insist upon honourifics and pronouns that match your legal gender, however - this may not be something you can change, and you may have to continue to live with it.

### Legally changing your name
{: #h-d15875ca-2b6f-412a-9d55-eb00e56f10b9}

<figure class="image"><a href="/assets/notion/social-transition/Kimi-no-Na-wa.jpg"><img src="/assets/notion/social-transition/Kimi-no-Na-wa.jpg" alt="" width="480" loading="lazy"></a></figure>

A deed poll is a common way people in this country indicate a change in their name or identity. Many people - cis and trans - do this often with life events (marriage and divorce come to mind). Some even change their names in an attempt to turn their life’s fortunes around.

You should pick a name that is comfortable for you, and that you should be somewhat sure that this is the name you want to go by. You’d have to go through the same process again if you change your mind in the future.

A deed poll should not cost more than \$60 - lawyers often charge a flat rate of \$50 with or without GST. Bring your own fancy paper, if you want to!

It goes without saying that you don’t need to be on HRT or go on surgery before changing your name with a deed poll. But you should feel safe enough to do so; physical correspondence (letters, packets) for banking services that you’ve signed up after your name change will come with your legal name. You can still address packages (from Shopee, Lazada) under your previous name if you live with family that isn’t accepting, and name changes do not happen automatically with most services (more below).

#### If you’re above 21
{: #h-a24a7f19-edb7-4d4f-8047-63a01d191af5}

Make an appointment at the lawyer and you’ll be asked to provide information, like a scanned version of your current IC. If you have a Chinese or Tamil name and wish to include them in your IC, you’ll have to provide this to the lawyer.

#### If you’re under 21
{: #h-a5974485-d98d-4fd7-ba3e-9cc5b6ec0768}

You’ll need both parents to approve of the name change. This means that your parents will be required to bring their IC, and you’ll have to bring your original birth certificates (or certificates of citizenships) and original NRICs.

Exceptions to the both parents rule would apply if:

- Both of your parents are divorced and if the consenting parent has full custody of you; if so, they would need to provide the divorce certificate and a Order of Court empowering them to unilaterally change your particulars
- If one of your parents have passed on. You’ll have to furnish a death certificate in this case.

#### After you’ve done your deed poll
{: #h-19ab9e73-e201-492f-b1da-bc32609c1f9b}

You’ll need to apply to update your particulars using the ICA [e-Service](https://www.ica.gov.sg/documents/ic/update_particulars). To do so, you’ll need:

<div class="indented" markdown="1">

- The deed poll in question
- A recent, passport-sized photo.

  If you are transitioning towards the gender you’re more comfortable with, it’s recommended that you present a passport-sized photo presenting as that gender, so it doesn’t look like there is a mismatch. Having a recent, passport-sized photo would help in applications to update your other documents, like your driver’s license or passport. Check [the ICA photo guidelines](https://www.ica.gov.sg/photo-guidelines) for more details.

  <aside class="callout callout-purple">
  <div class="callout-icon" aria-hidden="true">🚪</div>
  <div class="callout-body" markdown="1">

  There’s no need to bring your ID out all the time; if you have the Singpass app, you can use your digital NRIC, which doesn’t immediately show your gender marker, and automatically updates the photo based on your passport.

  </div>
  </aside>

</div>

You’ll need to pay an additional \$60 fee (by credit/debit or PayNow) as a processing fee.

It takes about 1-2 weeks for ICA to approve your application; officially they say 3 days. It’s advised that you check the portal to see if it’s approved, as they may not send a notification promptly.

After your application is complete, it’ll take another week to collect your IC. ICA only begins to properly update your information once you have collected your IC - this process can take hours or up to a day; updates on Singpass and Singpass-dependent databases can take up to 3-5 days after.

You can apply for a new passport and IC at the same time via the ICA portal.

If you want to update your driver’s licence, however, you’ll have to go direct to the Traffic Police Headquarters to replace your licence. Bring a physical copy of your passport-sized photo to do so; this will cost \$25.

#### Updating other information (bank accounts, insurance, etc)
{: #h-e8acedac-784d-4ad2-87da-d693e1d622df}

As most banks use information listed with SingPass and SingPass’ MyInfo database - changing your name with your bank may be simpler procedure (with the big local banks, DBS and OCBC) through your internet banking app.

You’ll have to physically go to the bank in order for them to issue you new credit/debit or ATM cards under your new name, however.

Do note that if you live with family (that may not be accepting) that any *update will roll over to physical correspondence* like letters. This doesn’t happen automatically until you update the bank, however.

Some insurers may require you to submit your deed poll; check with your insurance agent for more details.

Updating information with your hospital, however, may be simpler - present your new NRIC to the nurse on the date of your next appointment.

### Legally changing your gender marker
{: #h-7c6b698a-987a-4d11-a622-23c13f9910b5}

Changing your gender marker might be a long process depending on your nationality, and may be complicated and expensive, especially if you have had surgery.

#### Requirements as a Singaporean
{: #h-724a48ce-9802-464e-bedf-9480c58d6f1d}

To legally change your gender marker as a Singaporean, you’ll need to first get surgery. This is a process that can take years, and includes very heavy costs - often out of pocket. Insurers will not cover transition-related surgery, as they’re often deemed as elective.

In general, the requirement to get a gender-affirming surgery is to be on HRT for at least 1 year. We’ll have a guide towards getting a referral for gender-affirming surgery soon - you can ask your questions on Transcend if you have any!

#### As a non-Singaporean
{: #h-99369f71-1d6d-4bce-bafd-bc96ef799434}

You may have to check with authorities in your country if you can change your marker there. Some countries [may require similar processes](https://apnews.com/article/transgender-sterilization-e2cd525389eb17bf5201fa0fcbabdbf3) like ours, while others may have policies that differ by state or region. Either way, as your documents (your work permit, or PR) often follow your primary document - i.e. your passport. You can then legally marry someone who is legally the other gender.

### Socially transitioning at work
{: #h-e109508d-c028-41f5-96e8-e63df180272e}

Depending on your current career stage, you may have to deal with many different ordeals when you socially transition. Most employers will ask to see your IC, and you may have to out yourself to human resources at some point.

If your workplace has a good queer-friendly policy, it may be possible to transition while at your current job. SMEs may also be a good place, if your bosses or superiors are trans-friendly, but it largely depends on workplace culture.

If you’re seeking to change your name and appearance and you feel that your current workplace will not allow this, it might be good for you to seek another job.

Socially transitioning at your workplace might not be necessary until a year or two into your transition - that is largely when changes begin to show.

#### Workplace safety
{: #h-e4935c6c-2a77-4e55-9e43-3a96a8764d3f}

Queer-friendly policies are largely just that - policy. Remember that you are not beholden to your employer (unless you actually are with a scholarship).

It’s entirely possible that in some fields - like programming, software engineering, media and the arts, as well as academia and research - people are largely more welcoming towards us. It might be good to inform one or two people who are largely “safe” first, to see how the environment is like.

In sectors like public education, it may however be difficult for you to transition. It might be good to consider a new job in your sector (in private education, if you were trained as a teacher, for instance).

#### Transitioning at work
{: #h-b29c4100-2c01-4a9d-91eb-47537eb9e587}

Set a timeline for you to transition at work, if you can do so. Depending on your particular career path, you may have to update socials, keep LinkedIn updated, or update a portfolio page. You may also have to update systems and software, and keep from them from being broken.

If you’re transfeminine, you may also have to update HR on your reservist obligations and PES status.

#### Looking for a new job
{: #h-43c3c932-aee2-469f-9864-02c6629fbfb5}

Generally - the guide here is that if you are able to - only out yourself at the end of the job search if you have to. If you present well and are comfortably seen as the gender you’re most comfortable with \[but are not legally the gender you are\], it might not be necessary to inform HR until you are at a later stage of your job interview.

If you’ve just graduated and are looking for a new job, you may consider doing one of several things:

- <u><em>Using your new name on your CV or resume.</em></u> Employers do not need to see your legal name on your CV or resume, and so it is not necessary for you to have your legal name on it. You can explain that you are trans and have a different legal name at the end of the process. *You do not need to have a photo of you enclosed in your CV or resume.* This applies for your portfolio (if you have one).
- <u><em>Using your new name on your LinkedIn and other professional profiles</em></u>*.* Same as previous - you do not need your legal name until you have to present your identification.

Some part-time jobs in retail will accept your chosen name if it differs from your legal name! Feel free to ask (they might have no choice anyway, given the current job market).

*When do you tell HR you’re trans?*

Ideally, the best time to inform HR is when they are sure that they want you to be in this position. In a sense - if you’re the best candidate for the role - would it matter if you’re cis or trans? If it does matter to them - it would probably be unsafe for you to take on this role.

You could also opt to inform a recruiter at the start of the job interview process - largely because it might be a good way to gauge if the company is trans-friendly. Some do tell recruiters at the start of the process because they’re in the middle of transitioning, and may lean towards a presentation that is of their birth gender. Ultimately, it’s a sense of safety; your time is also precious, too, and transphobic employers are ultimately not worth your time. *You hold a lot of power as a worker; don’t forget that!*

If your university degree (or latest qualification) is in your previous name or birth name, you may have to include your deed poll when you send HR your degree or degree transcript.

### Socially transitioning in school
{: #h-92fd5317-1446-4d85-b0ad-058461274da0}

School systems generally vary in how they accept trans and queer students, and some school systems *may* allow you to socially transition without legally changing your name. MOE policy however (on the pre-university level) has generally been one of *not encouraging* queerness \[insert very loud booing, #FixSchoolsNotStudents\].

Some junior colleges may be more welcoming than others, with some accepting your preferred name/pronouns as well! We will not list these here, however; be sure to have an understanding of safety within your JC before you come out.

Universities and polytechnics are generally more accepting spaces, with the Inter-University LGBT Network a good space to start out if you need school-specific information about socially transitioning.

#### Changing your name with a pre-university or junior college
{: #h-f728372b-9891-4ab7-9940-d5495a9c001e}

Your name will be updated automatically after you collect your IC (when your Singpass updates) in the MOE system, but you’ll have to send your deed poll and NRIC to your teacher-in-charge as there will be other admin work to be done - you’ll have to have your name changed in the Cockpit system if you have exams that you need to register for.

Retroactive name changes - if you’ve already sat for an exam, for example, or if you’ve already had a certificate for your PSLE, N or O-Levels - will not be granted.

### Socially transitioning in life
{: #h-5260c9d7-e647-4538-837f-99a9c55665f8}

The hard part about changing your name is telling everyone about it. Depending on how you feel about being known as your previous name, how many friends you’d like to tell, or how you want people to know, you may choose to deal with this in different ways.

You can opt to compartmentalise information - if your current name is something that doesn’t resemble your current deadname at all, it might be good to build new social media accounts and networks. \[This might not work, if, say, you depend on them for work.\]

It goes without saying that you don’t need to be on HRT or even legally change your name before socially transitioning; a change of name is just that, a change! Transcend is usually a good space for you to try out new identities and new names.

#### Dealing with pre-transition photos on social media networks
{: #h-fd55e9cd-5efd-4e39-8ac2-72892ace8840}

It might be helpful for you to delete all instances of pre-transition photos on social media networks. If you would like to store them, a good place would be on a hard disk or thumb drive, or your phone (as long as you have appropriate privacy instances).

This is to largely prevent people from immediately outing you or knowing outside of your control. (It gets scarily easy to find previous photos of yourself, especially if your younger self was more loose with social media privacy.)

Most social media instances will have a function to download the information you have put up and to delete them from servers in total.

Note that you *must* do the above - if you wish to - before you formally announce your transition and come out in public.

#### Social media hygiene

This is the second most tedious thing here - trying to figure out who you’re comfortable with coming out to.

A basic rule of thumb is to <u>block anyone you do not want to come out to</u>. Optionally, you can disconnect (softblock or unfriend) <u>people who don’t need to know</u>. Most times, however, people won’t actually notice the change, and will generally keep to themselves if they do see it.

On Facebook, [blocking someone](https://www.facebook.com/help/1000976436606344) means that they will not have access to your profile at all. They will not be able to message you, nor see your profile on Messenger.

On Instagram, [blocking someone](https://help.instagram.com/426700567389543) (and the profiles they create) means that they will not be able to access your posts, but may see your bio. They will not be able to message you.

On Twitter, [blocking someone](https://help.twitter.com/en/using-twitter/blocking-and-unblocking-accounts) means that they will not be able to access your posts but may see your bio and that they blocked you. They will not be able to message you.

On TikTok, [blocking someone](https://support.tiktok.com/en/using-tiktok/followers-and-following/blocking-the-users) will mean that you are no longer visible to that person and that they will not be able to message you.

On LinkedIn, [blocking someone](https://www.linkedin.com/help/linkedin/answer/a1380117/block-a-member-overview?lang=en) will mean that you won’t be able to access their profile and they will not be able to message you.

On Telegram - blocking someone will mean that they will still be able to access your contact, but not your profile photo. They can no longer message you. They can still access your profile if they have your Telegram user handle.

On Whatsapp, [blocking someone](https://www.businessinsider.com/guides/tech/what-happens-when-you-block-someone-on-whatsapp) means that you can no longer receive messages or calls from them, and they cannot see your profile picture updates.
