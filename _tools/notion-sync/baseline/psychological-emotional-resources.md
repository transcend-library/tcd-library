### Introduction
{: #h-1ba1e4e0-d04c-4b84-a774-0821248e228d}

The nature of our identities and the way systems are structured, as well as the interpersonal nature surrounding our identities and the metaphysical nature of our selves means that psychological and emotional support is important and something we will interact with - if not briefly, then for a sustained manner.

Trans people get psychological care for various reasons: to figure themselves out; to gain support for their transition; to access medical transition itself. Still, knowing where to go is so difficult; most doctors don’t really know what to do; specialty doctors are few and far between and may be prohibitively costly if you are only seeking clarity for aspects of your identity or need long-term therapy with regards to aspects of your life, like trauma.

The document here assumes that you’re seeking assistance largely for gender-related issues, though related conditions and issues tied to our gender mean that we end up seeking psychological assistance for other diagnoses, like ADHD, OCD, autism and others.

Seeking help for your gender dysphoria might be the first step in investigating and understanding more of other conditions that may be present in you; most hospital systems will allow you to seek psychological help and will not withhold gender-affirming HRT when you do so. (Exceptions apply, like when the use of testosterone may interfere with antidepressants like selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors - SSRIs.)

#### Psychiatry, psychology, counselling and psychotherapy
{: #h-b7980115-87a7-433c-bd0e-14f28b43259c}

The casual person often refers to psychological medicine as a monolith, when there are fairly strict boundaries between psychiatrists, psychologists, counsellors and psychotherapists, and each have a different role in psychological medicine and psychological care. To recap, from [the guide to gender-affirming healthcare]({{site.baseurl}}/guide/gender-affirming-healthcare/#h-5657a66f-6ba7-4b79-8b43-5d873adb7950):

It’s likely that you’ll encounter all four of these at some point of your transition and your life path if you have some access to them. Most trans people will certainly have to access psychiatric care at some point, given how the pathway to gender-affirming care is structured.

#### The type of support you need
{: #h-2cb8627e-36be-43d8-87f0-4b4ed2b45d7d}

In general, when determining the type of professional support, consider what you would like to seek at the end. Ask yourself:

- Is there a particular end goal I have in mind when I seek psychological support? (A diagnosis of the problems I am experiencing, an understanding of my gender and who I am, or emotional support? Help in explaining what I am experiencing with someone I love?)
- Am I able to access this support on a regular basis? (This could be an issue of time, money as well as other issues)

Not everything that is available will be a magical solution for yourself; many times you will still have to put in work towards healing and working towards a better version of you. In short - psychological medicine isn’t a one-stop solution for all your problems.

Having a partner or loved one meet with a psychiatrist or therapist might help in allowing them to understand what you have been going through as well, especially if you are younger and need financial support over HRT and other related treatments. This, however, might not be something that will help entirely; there is a chance that they might still be unable to accept, or that acceptance will take a longer time to happen.

If you need help explaining what you feel to a parent, [I’ll Walk With You](https://www.congregaytion.com/ill-walk-with-you) contains a suitable summary and guide of what gender dysphoria is.

Medication may help in allowing you to function. As with all mental health interventions it is important for you to find out how you feel after having them; in some instances it may be possible for you to switch to another drug if you are suffering from adverse effects.

### Psychological medicine in public healthcare
{: #h-db96e90e-7dbc-42e5-9dc6-35e928e457a2}

If you’ve already gone through the gender-affirming care routes that are available in the guide, you’ll have some inkling of what is needed in order for you to acquire psychological care and treatment.

Some hospitals, like CGH, have trained psychologists who can help in talking therapy when it comes to issues like gender. At CGH, you’ll get an open appointment date that you can use to seek assistance with regards to other issues apart from gender, as well as help with talking therapy and psychotherapy.

We’re still not sure as to whether NUH or other hospitals provide psychological care. It is possible to get psychological care from other hospitals and to be assessed for other mental health conditions (like depression, ADHD and autism); the process to do so is largely similar to the process of getting a gender dysphoria diagnosis through a polyclinic.

It is also generally possible for you to see a psychologist at some polyclinics, or be referred to an IMH psychologist or psychiatrist.

If you want to be assessed for certain mental health conditions in public healthcare - do note that these will inadvertently show up on records, and while discrimination based on mental health conditions is illegal, it may continue to happen. If you can afford it and want to be assessed for a certain condition (like autism or ADHD) and do not want it to be formally placed on the record, then it may be better to seek out private practice.

### Psychological medicine in private healthcare
{: #h-368c1663-dd06-4ef4-94a1-08abaa39fc9a}

Private mental health treatment is often a faster and sometimes more affirming route that people can choose to take if they can afford it. While rates are significantly higher, the chance that you may be able to embark immediately on therapy or medication to treat the condition you have been experiencing is high.

Assessments for adult ADHD, for instance, may be way more difficult in public healthcare than private healthcare; public health psychiatrists will often pore over results from school and deem you able to function if you managed to survive our educational system, while private psychiatrists are more able to assess you holistically based on your own experience and how others have experienced you in terms of your condition.

There are also other benefits to private healthcare, especially in urgent situations; if you are experiencing thoughts of self-harm, conditions at IMH can be rather distressing, and nurses in public health are required to gender you by your legal gender and you may be warded individually (which is way more costly) or by your birth-assigned gender (which can be distressing).

### Counselling and psychotherapy
{: #h-88627bb7-1c4e-4851-8901-14c2ddf7ed7a}

In seeking assistance for wider issues related to gender, a counsellor or psychotherapist would be highly recommended; while we largely interact with psychiatrists for assistance with regards to our medical transition, the day-to-day nature of social transition and how we can deal with others’ perceptions means that it may be costly to repeatedly see a psychiatrist to seek support with regards to gender.

The inherent nature of gender also means that it will take time, rather than expert help, to determine and decide for yourself who you would want to be. A doctor can tell you that you have gender dysphoria, but it may be difficult for you to determine how you want to present, whether it is safe for you to present in a certain way, and what you want to be referred to without some degree of self-exploration and understanding of self.

Seeking counselling and psychotherapy will also help with dealing with some aspects of social transition, like transitioning with a partner, dealing with family, and transitioning at a workplace.

#### Best practices for counselling
{: #h-7d6ab0a4-1239-41fe-95f7-bfc34eb42daa}

<aside class="callout callout-blue">
<div class="callout-icon" aria-hidden="true"><img src="/assets/notion/psychological-emotional-resources/ralpoint.png" alt=""></div>
<div class="callout-body" markdown="1">

The following section is written from Ralsei’s perspective - reach out to them for more details on Transcend!

</div>
</aside>

Seeking a counsellor or psychotherapist may be difficult, and you may find yourself in a bind, at times. Each therapist will have a different approach to therapy, and some approaches may not be as useful to yourself as others. Some strategies I have felt worked for counselling and psychotherapy sessions include:

- *Being honest*. This means you should be open; therapists are trained not to judge you for your actions, and if you feel that there are some emotions - like shame - tied towards how you feel about a certain want or desire - being honest will allow a therapist to help you unravel and separate that desire from your own shame, or control that desire itself. Most of the therapists listed here can be trusted especially on gender issues.
- *Journal* - You don’t have to keep a diary around, but keep notes of what was discussed and what you have concluded at the end of each session. This helps - it makes what was talked about or discussed concrete, and you can refer back to it at every session.
- *Understand that no matter is too small* - if it affects you, it affects you. No matter is too small; you may be witnessing the start of a war (like me) and still be affected by someone misgendering you or the stress of people asking you about your name.
- *Understand that your therapist needs boundaries* - maintain a professional counsellor-patient relationship. Most therapists will have an agreement that you will sign before embarking on sessions. Take note of the boundaries listed down there, as well as the policies on patient confidentiality they will have; you may be allowed to email them in between sessions to seek guidance or just assistance.
- *Know when your therapist is not right for you* - sometimes you just don’t vibe well. My previous psychotherapist did not have a good understanding of gender, and thus I had to decide to change my therapist, because he was not helpful at that moment. Cost may be an issue - if you work well with your current therapist and still want to continue, you could opt to see the therapist less.
