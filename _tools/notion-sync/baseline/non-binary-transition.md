### Introduction to non-binary medical transition
{: #h-c529cc92-22b8-4dea-8b06-d1609ee2fa05}

The topic of a non-binary medical transition can be a tricky one - its approaches, while novel, is thoroughly rooted in the singular idea of gender identity, and how it may change with a greater clarity in your sense of self.

Both binary transition methods presume that you want to get to a certain stage - to be read as the opposite gender than what you were assigned to at birth. Medical specialists that you may meet may presume this as your goal; in a conversation with an endocrinologist, we were told that there was a possibility that as people progressed in their gender journeys they may decide to want a more binary transition, and that the hospital goal was largely to assist with an in-house assessment on what this may look like.

It doesn’t help that at times that medical specialists still do not have a good understanding of what non-binary transition looks like, and may be stuck on the conventional stereotypes of transness. This means that if you see a endocrinologist or psychiatrist that has had experience with binary trans folk - like Dr Tsoi - it’s very much likely that you may be rejected for being non-binary; you may also be started on a binary transition plan, which may not be what you’re looking for.

We don’t have much evidence on what a non-binary medical transition would look like in the context of the Singapore medical system. Most of the medication here - especially in AMAB non-binary transitioning methods - will be prescribed off-label if it is available at all; it is possible that you’ll be asked to microdose a binary hormone instead to facilitate masculinsation and feminisation. AFAB non-binary transitions will largely consist of a more surgical solution (with top surgery) and microdosing testosterone.

### Understanding what you want from a non-binary transition
{: #h-afe4e85b-1286-4a8e-aba8-4c0a3eb35341}

The most important thing from wanting a non-binary transition is largely asking yourself, perhaps, what you would like from your body.

This might be a difficult thing to do, largely because we often view our gender through various lenses - families, work, school and friends - and doing so often leads us into various ideas that perhaps being a non-binary person (or, in some contexts, a binary trans person) might be a choice that makes us better off.

Hence, it may take a while for you to figure out your choice. A non-binary identity - as one that largely defies the binary categorisations of the male and female - is something that many of us realise we need to craft and define on our own. In a medicalised setting, especially at the hospital with an endocrinologist and psychiatrist who usually see binary trans people, it will be difficult to explain the desire for partial masculinsation and feminisation.

Take some time, perhaps, to explore with your gender expression! Use a binder, or tuck; you can also use shoulder pads or packers and generally explore how you are dressed. You can perhaps choose to look through social media and discover aspects of others that you would like to incorporate with your expression, but understand that being non-binary is often a unique experience that defies categorisation.

With this exploration, perhaps, you can have a greater understanding of what you want for your body. Journal, perhaps, and write down what you want. The medicalised nature of how gender is often viewed means that we largely desire evidence of soundness of mind, a certainty that this is what we want, and the often experimental and off-label nature of medical transition - especially in AMAB non-binary people - means that we don’t know or understand if there are long term effects to selective estrogen receptor modulators (SERMs) like raloxifene, particularly if you change your mind later in transition.

You can take a look at the [psychological and emotional resource guide]({{site.baseurl}}/guide/psychological-emotional-resources/) for resources that you might be able to use to help in your journey; asking others on the server will also help!

The CGH gender clinic may direct you to their in-house psychologist, who may also be able to help you understand yourself as well.

<div class="callout-icon" aria-hidden="true">👉🏾</div>
<div class="callout-body" markdown="1">

<u>What happens if you change your mind? </u>

If you’re still considering transitioning to a binary gender, this should largely be okay, especially if you have been approved for medical transition. For AFABs, the option would be to increase your dose of T; for AMABs, you can simply drop your dose of raloxifene or your choice of SERM.

If you’re deciding to halt medical transition, this should also be okay - if you’re formally being supported by an endocrinologist, do consider options to wind down and halt your medication, as an immediate withdrawal can cause you to be in a state of emotional flux which can be dangerous, especially if you have a pre-existing psychological condition like depression.

You should be allowed to explore your gender without any guilt or shame tied to this exploration. Still, there will be some degree of judgement from people who consider this a phase. How you feel and who you are is ultimately up to you!

### AFAB non-binary transition
{: #h-f5d489b5-4bf5-477b-bcb5-df20896c74aa}

The AFAB non-binary transition - with goals towards partial masculinisation - will largely be centered around microdosing T and getting top surgery.

#### Microdosing testosterone
{: #h-f8a0314c-090d-4846-8d5d-94e9c554a50b}

Testosterone - or T - is the chief sex hormone in transmasculine medical transition and a chief driver in masculinisation. T has several delivery methods; the three most common available T agents are testosterone cypionate (via injection), testosterone undecanoate (Nebido, also via injection) and Androgel.

<div class="table-wrap"><table>
<tr><td><strong>Testosterone cypionate<br><br></strong>Testosterone cypionate is a slow-acting ester that is injectable and has a release time between 8 to 10 days. It is commonly administered in an intramuscular manner. T cypionate - namebrand Depo-Testosterone - is usually injected between once every week to once every three weeks.</td></tr>
<tr><td><strong>Testosterone undecanoate (Nebido)<br><br></strong>Testosterone undecanoate - with the brand name Nebido - is commonly administered in Singapore in an intramusclar manner, though it is also available elsewhere orally (a less effective pathway).</td></tr>
<tr><td><strong>Sustanon - testosterone propionate, testosterone phenylpropionate, testosterone isocaproate and testosterone decanoate<br><br></strong>Sustanon is a mix of four esters of T in arachis oil and benzyl alcohol. Each mililiter of the solution contains propionate of 30mg, phenylpropionate of 60mg, isocaproate of 60mg and decanoate of 100mg.</td></tr>
<tr><td><strong>Androgel</strong><br><br>Androgel is a clear, alcohol-based gel that contains 1% non-esterified T. It is fairly fast-acting once it has been absorbed by the skin, and will have to be applied 1-2 times daily to maintain T levels. Approximately 10% of the applied testosterone from the gel is absorbed into the system. <br><br>Note that Androgel should be applied to clean, dry skin and should not be applied to your genitals. Application sites should be allowed to dry for a few minutes before you put on any clothes, and you should wash your hands thoroughly with soap and water after application.<br><br>In order to prevent transfer to another person, clothing should be worn to cover the application sites. If direct skin-to-skin contact with another person is anticipated, the application sites should be washed thoroughly with soap and water. Users should wait at least 2 hours after applying before showering or swimming; for optimal absorption, it may be best to wait 5-6 hours.</td></tr>
</table></div>

It is possible to get an understanding psychiatrist to write a memo stating that you are a non-binary transmasculine person who wishes to start on a smaller dose of T. Still, you will have to ask your endocrinologist on an appropriate dosage. Most endocrinologists in the Singaporean public health system do understand to some level the needs of the non-binary person, and you should be able to negotiate for a lower dose.

T can often seem more “powerful” than your body’s natural hormones, and your mileage will thus vary. At a small initial dose you may get a lot more masculinisation or not at all; monitoring with an endocrinologist is hence very very important. Monitor how you feel in relation to what you’d like for yourself and your body; talk to others if you’d like!

For more on the effects of T itself, take a look at the [transmasculine guide to HRT]({{site.baseurl}}/guide/transmasculine-hrt/#h-7f399f4d-87e9-4ce0-8801-c27867f1a985).

#### Top surgery
{: #h-9fffee9c-0c3c-434f-9391-1afd42b66703}

Top surgery - or mastectomy - is a surgical procedure for the removal of your breast or chest growths. We’ve included top surgery in this section largely because a chief goal of non-binary transition in both AFAB and AMAB non-binary people is to have an androgynous body, and top surgery may be part of this goal.

For AFABs, an implicit requirement (that doesn’t seem to be WPATH standard with version 8 of its Standards of Care) has been that an AFAB individual must have had taken some masculinising HRT for a year.

It’s largely because of the potential shifts in body fat amongst people who eventually take T - an aesthetics issue - though some surgeons want a person to be on T for a year for them to confirm that this person is serious about transition and not acting on impulse.

If you do not have plans to go on T, however, the idea of having top surgery before might not be an issue; if you are clear about your transition goals and have been living as a non-binary person for a while, psychiatrists that are understanding might be able to write a referral explaining that.

Requirements for top surgery will be dependent on the surgeon you’re looking for; there is an excellent list of surgeons on Transcend’s #transmasc-medical channel [here](https://discord.com/channels/407556692822327298/1018830702369837056/1018830704718655569) \[requires access to Transcend\]. Some surgeons may not need any requirements before approving you for top surgery.

Available options are dependent on what is physically possible for yourself, and hence is something that you would have to consult with your endocrinologist or surgeon beforehand. Again, as this is a step that is largely irreversible, you should be very sure on what you want before embarking on top surgery.

You’ll be told the name of the procedure (so you can look it up). You can also ask for before and after photos to view results; all surgeons will have a portfolio so you can see if the results others have gotten under the surgeon are amenable to yourself.

Some surgeons do not require a gender dysphoria diagnosis in order for you to get top surgery; so it is possible entirely to get top surgery done without a psychiatrist’s memo if you’re above 21. To get there you’ll largely need a referral from a GP (claiming back pain, if you have large breasts) to a suitable surgeon (listed in the surgery guide).

Hypothetically - this is largely because insurers only see the name of the surgery that you’re claiming for, and the amount of tissue that was taken.

Do understand, however, that top surgery is irreversible, and you should be very, very certain before you embark down this path.

<u>Breast reduction</u>

Reducing the breast size is another available option - non-flat top surgery options may be available for you, depending on how your chest looks like and what is physically possible for yourself. You’ll need to remove a total of 500g of tissue or 250g from either side of the breast for it to be claimable by insurance.

Access to a breast reduction and top surgery might be dependent on the size of your breasts; it may be possible that access will be way harder if your breasts are deemed too small, or you may have to prove that you have gender dysphoria (by seeing a psychiatrist) in order for a breast reduction to be approved. Doctors are largely concerned about whether it has been an inconvenience to you in terms of back pain, rather than how you feel personally about your breasts.

Depending on the size of your breasts, you may be asked to return post-procedure to have fat placed back into your chest if you’re going for a breast reduction; this is largely because if you have large breasts your skin won’t be able to hold much weight when the breast tissue is removed.

### AMAB non-binary transition
{: #h-e9600dcc-d014-470b-9617-b21012c4f85c}

The transfeminine non-binary transition is partially more complicated than it seems, but don’t let that daunt you! The major hurdle here might perhaps be at the endocrinologist; most seem to believe that the binary transfeminine transition is what we end up wanting in the end, or do not understand the off-label use of the selective estrogen receptor modulators (SERMs) that we are asking for.

Generally, binary transfeminine HRT assumes a desire for us to be demasculinised in terms of sexual desire and function as well as fertility. While the impacts on fertility by a non-binary HRT are as yet unknown, some non-binary transition options may allow you to retain libido and sexual function; other options like application of a small dose of testosterone may help in retaining the sexual function of your genitals.

Still, access and monitoring here will be a major hurdle. We’ll focus here on what seems provably accessible at the moment in Singapore; if you’re keen on looking into the more expensive or experimental transition methods, do take a look at the [non-binary transfeminine therapy](https://transfemscience.org/articles/nonbinary-transfem-overview/) article on Transfeminine Science.

#### Low dose E and anti-androgen therapy
{: #h-67b9f7a5-75b7-42ca-8c6f-0eca5a36c7ba}

The easiest way to perhaps transition towards a more non-binary body shape is perhaps starting out with demasculinisation. A low dose of estrogen (or E - around 2mg of oral estradiol a day) and an appropriate low dose of anti-androgen (AA) to half (or even zero out your T levels) may help in demasculinisation and allow for some feminisation.

E2 dose equivalents can be found in [this Transfeminine Science article](https://transfemscience.org/articles/e2-equivalent-doses/).

As with most HRT methods, picking and choosing what you’d want is largely improbable or very difficult. If you’re picking an AA that is also a progestin like cyproterone, there may be a chance that you may experience more breast growth than normal. Most transfeminine people tend to have poor or suboptimal breast development relative to cisgender women, so you shouldn’t expect top growth at a marked degree when you begin at a low dose. Top growth will not continue even as hormones are withdrawn.

As always, monitor how you feel! Operating at a low dose of T and E may mean that you might experience some degree of lethargy. Monitor the top growth that you’re experiencing as well, and adjust or talk to your endocrinologist when you need to.

For more on the effects of E, go to the library article on the [transfeminine guide to HRT.]({{site.baseurl}}/guide/transfeminine-hrt/#h-bd64ad1e-1700-45f0-8dce-dcca645819dc)

#### A selective estrogen receptor modifier, low dose of E and an anti-androgen
{: #h-7187f2bf-13be-42a2-b24c-8799347f1b51}

<aside class="callout callout-purple">
<div class="callout-icon" aria-hidden="true"><img src="/assets/notion/non-binary-transition/ralpoint.png" alt=""></div>
<div class="callout-body" markdown="1">

It’s important for us here at the Transcend Library to point out that many of the drugs listed here are experimental at best when used on AMABs. Their effects, while explored extensively in elderly men and cisgender women, may not be well-understood for the non-binary AMAB.

*Transfeminine Science* has updated studies on the use of non-binary transition methods for transfeminine people, which you can find [here](https://transfemscience.org/articles/nonbinary-transfem-overview/#update-1). Much of this section is dependent on the information that we’ve gathered both from the *Transfeminine Science* article and from our own research into what’s available.

If you choose to self-manage and experiment, ensure that you have some access to a doctor beforehand. **Close monitoring per advice from your endocrinologist is what we highly recommend here.**

</div>
</aside>

Selective estrogen receptor modifiers (SERMs) are used to prevent breast development. The most common SERM used in this case - and the one we’ll chiefly discuss here - is raloxifene.

Raloxifene largely acts as an estrogenic compound in some tissue while acting as an anti-estrogen in others (namely breast tissue); it’s mainly estrogenic in bones, most bodily tissue and most organs, but is anti-estrogenic in breast tissue and bio-female reproductive tissue. As such, feminisation of other body parts can continue to occur which may help facilitate the goals you would want for your body.

Still, raloxifene will cause some discomfort - hot flashes and some breast growth might be expected, and your breasts may look less like gynaecomastia and more like a cis woman’s; some fat distribution will also be expected around the upper and lower abdomen.

Raloxifene will help reduce bone density loss but is not as effective as estrogen or testosterone itself in maintaining bone density. Thus, it may be needed for you to supplement your dose with a low dose of E; the body is not meant to operate on low sex hormones.

In AMABs SERMs will greatly increase T levels if you do not use an AA (because of an imbalance in the [hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal axis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypothalamic%E2%80%93pituitary%E2%80%93gonadal_axis)) - on average up to 2 times or 2.5 times according to Transfeminine Science. You’ll need to have an appropriate AA as well - bicalutamide is not recommended because of how it is a competitive antagonist of the androgen receptor, and it might be possible that your T levels become too high for bicalutamide to block.

Hence - **close monitoring per advice from your endocrinologist is what we highly recommend here**. We have had <u>some indication</u> that raloxifene may be prescribed for off-label use at CGH \[though it may not be immediately available as they do not have it in stock\]; if you do want to self-manage this, you should start going to an endocrinologist or start yourself on the path of getting yourself to a gender clinic.

The effects of SERMs, E and anti-androgens are also contingent on how you feel about yourself and the feminisation or partial feminisation/demasculinsation that you will go through. Journal and record down these thoughts; explore your own gender expression and work towards your own body goals. These things may bring some clarity as to whether you want a binary transfeminine transition or if you want to continue with a non-binary hormone regime.

<aside class="callout callout-blue">
<div class="callout-icon" aria-hidden="true">🍵</div>
<div class="callout-body" markdown="1">

The body is not meant to operate on low or no sex hormones, and it is likely that you will experience some form of exhaustion or brain fog if you have completely suppressed T and have had no or low amounts of E.

The dangers to bone health too will be evident. Minimal or no bone density loss can occur with relatively low estradiol levels of 30 to 50 pg/mL (110 to 183.56 pmol/L). But significant bone density loss will occur with estradiol levels of 20 to 30 pg/mL (73.42 to 110.14 pmol/L).

While bone density loss is possibly reversible, it is something that can have costly consequences and be very dangerous in the long term. Supplement your bone health with calcium, vitamin D and bisphosphonates.

</div>
</aside>

<u>Dosages on SERMs</u>

If your chief reason to get on raloxifene is to stop top growth, a recommended dosage might be as follows:

1. Initial dosage on estradiol to 2mg oral per day (sublingual is not recommended as the absorption is higher, overpowering the effects of raloxifene) - this is what is largely considered a microdose of E
2. Increase estradiol to less than 4mg oral a day and add raloxifene to up to 120mg a day keeping to a 2mg E to 60mg raloxifene ratio. The ratio has worked anecdotally amongst transfeminine enbys in not allowing E to overpower raloxifene. As usual - seek expert advice and ensure that you have at least some access to blood tests. Feminisation may occur here.
3. Keep cyproterone acetate at 12.5 mg a day and reduce as needed.

<u>Blood monitoring on SERMs</u>

If you’re intending to get blood tests and can only self-manage, it is recommended that you take regular blood tests, per the regiment below:

### Binding safely
{: #h-292905a4-4f38-45bd-80cf-d07fdebc669d}

<aside class="callout callout-pink">
<div class="callout-icon" aria-hidden="true">🩹</div>
<div class="callout-body" markdown="1">

This guide is mostly based off Hudson’s FTM Resource Guide on binding, [seen here.](http://www.ftmguide.org/binding.html)

</div>
</aside>

In the hot and humid climate that deeply characterises and permeates our experience in Singapore, binding might seem like a daunting and yet necessary task that non-binary people need to get through, for AFABs who have gone through a first puberty and for AMABs who have gotten through a binary transfeminine transition or is going through one.

Binding should **not** hurt, cut your skin or prevent you from breathing. Do not buy a binder that is one size too small in the hopes that it will flatten your chest - binders are already designed to be very tight when they fit properly, and getting one that’s too small will cause severe discomfort and injury.

You should not spend more than 8 hours binding as a rule of thumb. Let your body get used to the binder slowly - spend two hours first, then four, then six - and remember to take breaks. Some people might be able to handle wearing a binder for very long; while others can’t make it to 8 hours - it is highly dependent on your body!

If you’re expecting top growth as an AMAB transfeminine person, you should not spend an excessive amount of time binding. Binding may risk changing the shape of your breast tissue, and binding for a long period of time (months or years) may change it substantially, causing your breasts to become flatter and less firm. This may not be ideal (unless that’s what you want).

Naturally, don’t wear a binder while doing strenuous activity or doing anything that requires a lot of lung effort as you’ve basically compressed your lungs. Do not wear them while choir singing, playing a wind instrument, or when sick with a respiratory illness; you will get out of breath easily and it will be difficult to catch your breath.
