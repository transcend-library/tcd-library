### An introduction to female hormones
{: #h-b30555a3-8756-4775-854a-177eef359836}

Estrogen is the chief sex hormone used in transfeminine hormone therapy. A person’s hormonal profile is dependent on the gonads they were born with; natal males have testes while natal females have ovaries.

As most of us on transfeminine hormone therapy have testes, the ideal goal of hormone therapy is to decrease the effects of testosterone (T) and dihydrotestosterone (DHT) so the feminizing effects of estrogen occur.

The major estrogen in the body is estradiol (E2, or E) and the main progestogen is progesterone (P4, or P). Both of them are responsible for and determine the body’s secondary sex characteristics. Estrogens cause feminization, which includes breast development, a softening of the skin, a feminine pattern of fat distribution, a widening of the hips, and other physical changes.

Progestogens have no known role in feminisation, but have important effects in the female reproductive systems and are essential hormones during pregnancy. They have an anecdotal effect on sexual desire and function, though this has not been well-proven.

In addition to their effects on the body, sex hormones have actions on the brain - influencing cognition, emotions and behaviour. Androgens produce pronounced sexual desire and arousal, including (oft unwanted) spontaneous erections in people who have penises, while estrogens appear to be the major hormones responsible for sexual desire in women.

Each person will experience varying degrees of change to transfeminine hormone therapy; some will have more changes than others, while others may not see changes for a while. Be patient with your own body as you navigate a second puberty; while you technically can’t overdose on estrogen, a high dose will impact prolactin, liver and kidney function. Feminisation can occur on a moderate dose and at moderate levels, and you do not need to take a high dose in order to kickstart your second puberty.

#### Expected changes from transfeminine hormone therapy
{: #h-bd64ad1e-1700-45f0-8dce-dcca645819dc}

Every person’s body is ultimately different, and this timeline of expected changes will vary significantly between individuals due to factors like genetics, diet and hormone levels as well as hormone regimen. In gender-affirming hormone therapy, adult or higher amounts of hormones are generally administered; this means that your second puberty is going to be slightly faster.

Some changes will be permanent. Many of us stop HRT for varied reasons - an inability to socially transition, adverse medical effects, or a desire to detransition. As such, before going on HRT, you should be aware of the permanent and reversible changes:

Some masculinisation cannot be reversed without external intervention; while facial and body hair can be thinned through transfeminine hormone therapy, permanent removal of facial hair can only occur with laser hair removal and electrolysis. Skeletal changes - such as widening and rounding of the pelvis or height changes - may not occur after growth plate closure after puberty.

Other effects of a masculine puberty may also be very difficult to reverse without surgical intervention, such as facial feminisation surgery, a tracheal shave, as well as breast augmentation and bottom surgery.

As mentioned above - be patient with your own body. It is normal to feel as though you aren’t making progress; we are, after all, our own worst critics.

#### Lab tests
{: #h-d674ec9d-2d99-45b4-815a-e116c5a638f7}

To monitor your health, doctors will perform a variety of tests in the lab. Labs are useful as it is an objective snapshot of your hormone levels, and diagnosing issues and providing assistance. It is important to confirm that suppression of T has occurred to prevent further masculinization.

You should get a blood screen at your trough - this means right before your next dose. This may be impossible to schedule in some instances (at the hospital, for example), but it should still be done to gauge what your E levels are at their lowest and if you should adjust your dose.

Blood tests are recommended once every 3-4 months; in the public health system testing occurs once every four months and may decrease depending on how long you have been on HRT.

These tests include the following:

<div class="table-wrap"><table>
<tr><td>Sex hormone testing</td><td>The most common tests that you’ll be put through to monitor the efficacy of HRT include a test of total estradiol levels as well as, testosterone levels. <br><br>Levels of free testosterone, free estradiol (E2), estrone (E1), dihydrotestosterone (DHT), follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH), luteinising hormone (LH) and sex-hormone-binding globulin are also measured to provide a further snapshot, though they are not entirely necessary. For a list of what is needed, take a look at the recommended testing regimens below.</td></tr>
<tr><td>Complete blood cell count</td><td>The complete blood cell count measures the amount of blood cells in your blood. An excess of red blood cells thickens the blood, impeding its passage through small blood vessels and causing a number of potential health problems, like thrombosis, haemorrhage and heart failure. <br><br>A complete blood count (CBC) test usually measures the following:<br>- Number of red blood cells (RBCs)<br>- Number of white blood cells (WBCs)<br>- Total amount of hemoglobin (HGB) in the blood<br>- The fraction of the blood composed of red blood cells (hematocrit or HCT)<br>- The mean corpuscular volume (MCV-- the size of the red blood cells, derived from MCV = HCT/HGB)</td></tr>
<tr><td>Liver function test</td><td>A liver function test looks into alterations in liver function from transfeminine HRT. Some anti-androgens - like bicalutamide and cyproterone acetate - will affect liver function<br><br>Common tests that measure for specific enzymes include: AST (aspartate aminotransferase, sometimes also called SGOT), ALT (alanine aminotransferase, sometimes also called SGPT), ALP (alkaline phosphatase). GGT (gamma glutamyl transferase) tests are not necessary in transfeminine people.</td></tr>
<tr><td>Kidney function tests</td><td>Renal panel tests - especially tests on potassium and urea levels - are key for transfeminine people taking spironolactone as there may be an increased risk of hyperkalemia - high potassium levels. <br><br>Other electrolytes are often tested in kidney function tests, like bicarbonate, chloride, creatinine, glucose and sodium.</td></tr>
<tr><td>Lipid profile test</td><td>A lipid profile test helps determine risk of heart disease. It includes total cholesterol, HDL (good) cholesterol, LDL (bad) cholesterol and triglyceride testing.</td></tr>
</table></div>
