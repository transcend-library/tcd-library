#### An introduction to testosterone
{: #h-87172273-2146-4a40-9090-e0b9cefc2236}

Testosterone (or T) is the chief sex hormone used in transmasculine medical transition, for people assigned female at birth. Like estradiol in transfeminine hormone therapy, T has several delivery methods:

<div class="indented" markdown="1">

- Injection (most common for T)
- Transdermal application via gel, cream or patches (Androgel being the most common application)
- Orally or sublingually (less common, as it will be straining on the liver)
- via implant (very uncommon)

</div>

The three most common (and available) T agents are testosterone cypionate (via injection), testosterone undecanoate (commonly called Nebido, and is an injectable), or Androgel.

Every person will experience varying degrees of change to testosterone hormone therapy; some will have more changes than others, while others may not see changes for a while. Be patient with your own body as you navigate a second puberty - if you stack (with another steroid) or increase your dose, you run the risk of your body breaking down the extra T in your body with an enzyme called aromatase. Taking more than your prescribed dose greatly increases related health risks - with your heart and liver.

Transmasculine people don’t need to repress the estrogen their body naturally makes, as T is often enough to cause masculinisation in general (this is unlike transfeminine therapy, where an anti-androgen is often taken to repress T).

#### Expected changes from T
{: #h-7f399f4d-87e9-4ce0-8801-c27867f1a985}

Getting T means that you’ll develop secondary sexual characteristics similar to those of men. These changes aren’t going to happen overnight — they’ll take months to be noticeable and will continue to develop over a period of years.

<div class="columns">
<div class="column" markdown="1">

<u>Some of these effects include:</u>

- Changes to skin - increased oiliness and potential acne, with skin becoming rougher in feeling and appearance
- Coarser hair - hair becoming thicker, and potential hair loss around the temples and crown for a more masculine hairline
- Increased body hair growth - on arms, legs, chest, belly and back
- Body fat redistribution to a more “masculine” pattern - fat shifting from hips, thighs and buttocks to the abdomen area
- Changes to body odors
- Facial hair growth
- Clitoral growth
- Vaginal dryness
- Cessation of periods
- An increased sex drive

</div>
<div class="column" markdown="1">

<u>Other effects - reported anecdotally - include:</u>

- A more angular jaw and face (with facial fat redistribution)
- Increase in energy level and appetite
- Increase in the size of feet and width/thickness of hands, potentially because of growth of cartilage/muscle/connective tissue
- Slight decrease in density of fatty breast tissue. (T will most likely not significantly decrease breast size - top surgery will be required for most trans men to remove breast tissue)
- Emotional changes - it is difficult to predict what emotional or psychological changes T will cause. Some may report that they’re more even-tempered; others may report that they are more short-tempered.

  It is important to log how you feel and discuss with an endocrinologist (if you have access to one) if your dosage needs to be changed.

</div>
</div>

Views on transition and whether it’ll bring a wholesale change in your personality are varied, but while testosterone may affect certain moods and feelings, it is generally not thought to be something that will magically change who you are at your core. Medical transition is usually a life-changing experience, however, and you should definitely experience some changes - both good and bad - taking T for the first time.

If you’re early in your gender journey, it may be useful for you to log these changes somewhere and journal.

<u><strong>Frequently asked questions</strong></u>

*Can I decide what changes I will get?*

<div class="indented" markdown="1">

It is impossible to pick which changes you’ll get, just as it is impossible to predict exactly when they will occur and to what degree. While results are influenced by genetic make-up or heredity, looking at your AMAB relatives can still be an inaccurate indicator - even cis people cannot pick what happens during puberty. There is a wide range of characteristics in everyone, and it is impossible to predict what an individual will develop in their second puberty.

Be somewhat prepared to socially transition once you get on T, if you haven’t had plans to do so - the timeframe and range of expected effects might surprise you!

</div>

*How soon to expect changes?*

<div class="indented" markdown="1">

The speed of change will differ based on several variables - dosage, intervals, delivery method, and the body’s sensitivity to T. Individuals may see differing results with the same dosage over the same period of time.

Most major changes should set in between 2-5 years, though many continue to report changes for years to come.

</div>

*If I double my dose, will changes happen twice as fast?*

<div class="indented" markdown="1">

Doubling your dose might actually *slow* your changes. Excess testosterone detected in the body is converted (aromatized by an enzyme called aromatase) into estrogen to maintain hormonal balance, thus very large doses would actually be counterproductive.

Large doses will also negatively affect your heart and liver health, and you should not change or sharply increase your dose without consulting your doctor.

</div>

*How often should I get my T levels checked?*

<div class="indented" markdown="1">

In the first year, you should have your levels checked once every 3-4 months. If you are self-managing your HRT, you can go to several GPs - like [Dr Pereira](https://manadr.com/manadr-clinic) or [Dr Yao](https://www.healthhub.sg/directory/14/65558/yao-medical-clinic) - to get checked. After the first year, you should get your bloods checked once every six months; when your levels are stable, this frequency can extend to a year.

</div>

#### Permanent and reversible changes
{: #h-cc089ac3-89d1-4a2a-a92f-b04e757f1321}

People choose to stop taking T for many reasons - a desire to have children, an inability to socially transition, adverse medical effects, or a desire to detransition. As such, before taking T, you should be aware of the permanent and reversible changes:

<div class="table-wrap"><table>
<tr><td><strong>Reversible changes</strong></td><td><strong>Permanent changes</strong></td></tr>
<tr><td><u>RBC counts and cholesterol levels<br><br></u>If the ovaries are functioning, it will probably revert to levels that are closer to pre-T levels.</td><td><u>Voice</u><br><br>Should stay at the pitch that it has reached at the point T therapy is stopped.</td></tr>
<tr><td><u>Muscle or fat changes</u><br><br>If one’s ovaries are functioning, it will revert back to more female patterns. <br><br>If the individual does not have a large body fat percentage to begin with and remains trim, a return to female fat patterns won't be as dramatic.</td><td><u>Facial and body hair</u><br><br>The hair that has come in at the point of stopping T will continue to grow in, but large quantities of new hair will most likely not appear.<br><br>For instance, if one has developed a patchy beard, it will retain that patchy state upon stopping T. Hair that has developed will grow back after shaving. <br><br>The texture or growth of hair might change depending on whether one’s ovaries are still producing estrogen, but hair that has developed will not disappear at once. <br><br>Electrolysis would be required for permanent hair removal.</td></tr>
<tr><td><u>Menstrual cycle</u><br><br>If the ovaries are functioning, periods will return.</td><td><u>Clitoral growth</u><br><br>Clitoral size and thickness will not shrink.</td></tr>
<tr><td><u>Skin oils and acne</u><strong><u><br><br></u></strong>May lessen as one switches to a E-dominant body</td><td><u>Hair loss (male pattern baldness)</u><br><br>If head hair has been lost - it most likely will not grow back upon stopping T.<br><br>Different anecdotal reports regarding the rate of hair loss have been reported; some say that hair loss stopped immediately after they stopped taking T, while others say that head hair continued to fall after stopping.</td></tr>
<tr><td><u>Body scent</u><br><br>May change.</td><td></td></tr>
<tr><td><u>Sex drive<br><br></u>Will decrease.</td><td></td></tr>
</table></div>

Starting and stopping hormone therapy will be a big decision that will in part perhaps mean a resurgence of dysphoria as your body is forced to adjust to changes. You should weigh these carefully with your endocrinologist before starting or ending hormone treatment.

#### Lab tests
{: #h-189a9997-ca49-480f-9ecc-86b82e0b502c}

To monitor your health, doctors will perform a variety of tests in the lab - mostly a test on your testosterone levels, a blood cell count, a liver function test, clotting factor test and lipid profile test. For more on these tests, take a look at [FTM Testosterone Therapy and General Health](http://www.ftmguide.org/tandhealth.html).

<div class="table-wrap"><table>
<tr><td>Test name</td><td>Description</td></tr>
<tr><td>Serum total T test</td><td>A serum total testosterone test measures the total of bound and free T in your system - <em>bound</em> meaning T binded to a protein (sex hormone binding globulin or albumin) and <em>free </em>meaning the active form of testosterone that binds to androgen receptor sites on cells.<br><br>Normal test levels for the serum total T test range anywhere between 300-1100 ng/dl (nanograms per deciliter) or 11.01-40.38 nmol/L (nanomoles per liter). Note that reference values listed for tests will vary from lab to lab. <br><br>Every body will have a different sensitivity to androgens, and so this number will not necessarily indicate results in terms of masculinisation. These levels are merely a guideline for you and your doctor to measure progress.</td></tr>
<tr><td>Complete blood cell count</td><td>The complete blood cell count measures your blood cells to see if some potential side effects of T have been occurring, including increased red blood cell count, increased haemoglobin, and increased hematocrit levels, all of which may indicate potential polycythemia.<br><br>Polycythemia is an abnormally high level of red blood cells. An excess of red blood cells thickens the blood, impeding its passage through small blood vessels and causing a number of potential health problems, like thrombosis, haemorrhage and heart failure.<br><br>A complete blood count (CBC) test usually measures the following:<br>- Number of red blood cells (RBCs)<br>- Number of white blood cells (WBCs)<br>- Total amount of hemoglobin (HGB) in the blood<br>- The fraction of the blood composed of red blood cells (hematocrit or HCT)<br>- The mean corpuscular volume (MCV-- the size of the red blood cells, derived from MCV = HCT/HGB)<br><br>Normal CBC levels for males are as below - do note that reference values listed for tests will vary from lab to lab. <br><br><strong>RBC (males):</strong> 4.7 to 6.1 million cells/ul (cells per microliter)<br><strong>WBC:</strong> 4,500 to 10,000 cells/ul<br><strong>Hemoglobin (males):</strong> 13.8 to 17.2 gm/dl (grams per deciliter)<br><strong>Hematocrit (males):</strong> 40.7 to 50.3%<br><strong>MCV:</strong> 80 to 95 femtoliter</td></tr>
<tr><td>Liver function test</td><td>A liver function test looks into alterations in liver function from testosterone therapy (though changes may not be entirely be from T alone). <br><br>Common tests that measure for specific enzymes include: AST (aspartate aminotransferase, sometimes also called SGOT), ALT (alanine aminotransferase, sometimes also called SGPT), ALP (alkaline phosphatase), and GGT (gamma glutamyl transferase). <br><br>Other tests that are used to evaluate liver function include: Albumin, Bilirubin, and PT (prothrombin time, an evaluation of clotting in the blood).</td></tr>
<tr><td>Clotting factor test</td><td>If you have an abnormal PT (prothrombin time) test, a clotting factor test is done to determine which coagulation factors have been affected. <br><br>T has been reported to suppress some clotting factors, and increase the anticoagulant effects of medication like Warfarin.</td></tr>
<tr><td>Lipid profile test</td><td>A lipid profile test helps determine risk of heart disease. It includes total cholesterol, HDL (good) cholesterol, LDL (bad) cholesterol and triglyceride testing.<br><br>Increased levels of T may contribute to the elevation of LDL (bad) cholesterol, which is why this should be monitored.</td></tr>
</table></div>

For reference ranges for all laboratory providers in Singapore, take a look at the blood test reference range document [here](https://docs.google.com/spreadsheets/d/143mMN6wdRRfaISQFDVFldLmYTdPcg_sc/edit?usp=sharing&ouid=110576942819404182187&rtpof=true&sd=true). Blood tests often go by the legal gender (or the gender that hospital systems have assigned to you).
