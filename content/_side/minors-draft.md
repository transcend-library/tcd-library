The Ministry of Health has national treatment guidelines (dated July 2025) for children and adolescents with gender dysphoria. In short: **if you are under 21, the route to hormones or surgery is now very narrow, but psychological support is available at any age.**

The guidelines use two age groups:

- **Children:** before puberty starts (before *Tanner stage 2*, the first physical signs of puberty).
- **Adolescents:** from the start of puberty until you turn **21**, the age of majority in Singapore.

Wherever the guidelines say "parents", this includes single parents and legal guardians.

### At a glance
{: #h-minors-at-a-glance}

| | Psychological support | Puberty blockers | Hormone therapy (HRT) | Surgery |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Before puberty** | Yes | No | No | No |
| **Puberty to 17** | Yes | No | No | No |
| **18 to 20** | Yes | No | Only in exceptional circumstances, with a Treatment Review Panel's agreement | Only in exceptional circumstances, with a Treatment Review Panel's agreement |

For most people, the guidelines expect decisions about medical transition to be made by the person themselves **after they turn 21**.

**Puberty blockers are not recommended at any age.** The guidelines say there is not enough high-quality evidence on their safety or effectiveness.

### What you can get at any age
{: #h-minors-any-age}

- **Assessment and diagnosis** of gender dysphoria, by a team rather than a single doctor where possible.
- **Psychological support:** individual therapy, family sessions, and group therapy (which can help if you don't have support at home or from friends). The goals of any therapy should be explained to you, and you should agree to it; the guidelines say therapy without your consent and involvement isn't recommended.
- **Help with other things you're going through,** like anxiety, depression, bullying, stigma, school stress or friendships.
- **Support for your parents,** including separate sessions if they're not yet accepting.
- **Help with school.** The guidelines say accommodations at school (and other organisations) should be worked out together between you, your family and the school.
- **Information** about your options, what to expect, and effects on fertility.

### Getting assessed
{: #h-22b2690f-21a7-4913-a25b-c63c59555b28}

The assessment is thorough and can take a long time; published data from specialist centres show **12 to 18 months**. It's usually led by a psychiatrist working with psychologists, counsellors or social workers. In public hospitals this is often a team; in private clinics, a psychiatrist may work alone or with other independent professionals.

Expect to talk about:

- how you've felt about your gender over time, including in childhood, and how puberty has affected that;
- your family, school and social life, and any difficult experiences;
- any social transition you've done so far, and how people reacted.

**Both of your parents will usually be interviewed too,** about what they've observed, their views on gender, and how decisions are made in your family. If your parents disagree with each other, the team will do a further family assessment before reaching a diagnosis or making recommendations.

The team will also check for other conditions that can overlap with gender dysphoria or affect how it's understood, such as autism, depression, anxiety, PTSD, eating disorders or body dysmorphia. Any other condition should be treated too. You may be asked to fill in questionnaires (for example, the *Utrecht Gender Dysphoria Scale – Gender Spectrum* or the *Gender Identity/Gender Dysphoria Questionnaire for Adolescents and Adults*).

A diagnosis doesn't automatically mean medical treatment. The guidelines say every plan should be individual and look at you as a whole person.

#### For children (before puberty)

- No medical treatment is given before puberty. Support is psychological and family-based.
- Assessment involves joint sessions with the child and parents, over **at least 6 months to a year**.
- On social transition, the guidelines say there is no conclusive evidence either way. Families are encouraged to talk openly about it; where parents are against it, professionals may help them find a middle ground (for example, in certain situations only).
- The guidelines also ask professionals to support parents in letting the child's gender identity develop freely, including returning to one that matches their sex assigned at birth.
- As the child approaches puberty, care should move smoothly to adolescent services.

### If you're 18 to 20: the exceptional route
{: #h-4d93e0bf-6ffb-4d23-9c96-0be896ea0874}

Hormones or surgery can only be offered if **all** of these are true:

1. You are **over 18**.
2. There is **clear evidence of benefit or harm reduction**, which may include medical or mental health grounds.
3. A **Treatment Review Panel (TRP)** has agreed.
4. **You and your parents** have given signed, informed consent.

There are no fixed criteria; each case is looked at individually, with your overall wellbeing and safety as the main consideration. As an example, the guidelines mention adolescents who were already assessed and supported for gender dysphoria as children.

#### The Treatment Review Panel
{: #h-minors-trp}

The TRP is a panel of professionals who decide, by consensus, whether hormones or surgery should be part of your treatment plan. At minimum, it includes:

- your psychiatrist;
- the psychologists, counsellors or social workers involved in your care;
- your endocrinologist or paediatrician;
- a surgeon, if surgery is being considered; and
- one more specialist who isn't involved in your treatment.

If you're likely to need an adult endocrinologist soon, one should be invited too. **You and both parents may be invited into the meeting.** If HRT is agreed, your endocrinologist or paediatrician will tell you; if surgery is agreed, your surgeon will.

The panel will look at things like why you want to transition medically, what might happen to your mental health if you don't, whether it may reduce your dysphoria, timing around milestones like **National Service, school or work**, and how accepting the people around you are likely to be.

They'll also check that you're able to make the decision (your **capacity**). This means showing that you:

- understand gender dysphoria and how it affects your life;
- understand the treatment, including realistic outcomes, which changes are permanent, and what it means for fertility;
- can weigh up the risks and benefits;
- aren't under pressure from other people or information, in person or online; and
- don't have a mental health condition that's affecting your decision-making.

Things to know about the TRP:

- **Its decision isn't binding on other doctors.** If one panel agrees to HRT and you later see a different endocrinologist, they don't have to prescribe the same treatment.
- **How meetings are run and charged varies** between doctors and hospitals. Ask about this up front.
- **Treatment continues to be reviewed.** If you start HRT or have surgery, your condition, and whether you and your parents still want to continue, will be checked regularly. Any major change goes back to the panel.

#### Consent from your parents
{: #h-minors-consent}

Signed informed consent is needed from **you and your parents** before any treatment starts.

- If one parent **can't be contacted** (for example, they live apart from you or refuse contact), consent from one parent is accepted, as long as a reasonable effort was made to reach the other.
- If neither parent can be contacted, your **legal guardian** can consent.

#### If HRT is agreed
{: #h-minors-hrt}

- HRT is started and managed by an **endocrinologist**, only after puberty and from age 18.
- **Before starting,** expect a full check-up (medical, family and medication history, a physical exam) and blood tests: blood count, kidney and liver function, hormone levels, prolactin (for transfeminine HRT), cholesterol and blood sugar, calcium and vitamin D, plus a bone density (DXA) scan and possibly a bone age X-ray.
- **Doses start low and are increased gradually,** to mimic a natural puberty.
  - *Transfeminine HRT:* oestradiol (tablets or patches), with an anti-androgen (spironolactone or cyproterone) or a GnRH agonist to lower testosterone.
  - *Transmasculine HRT:* testosterone injections every two weeks.
- **Monitoring** until your "puberty" on HRT is complete: height, weight and blood pressure every 3 months; hormone levels and vitamin D every 6 months (plus prolactin for transfem HRT, and haematocrit and cholesterol for transmasc HRT); and a bone density scan every year. After that, monitoring is the same as for adults.
- **Fertility** must be discussed with you and both parents before starting, including the chance that fertility is affected long-term, and options to preserve it (such as sperm banking or egg freezing, which are regulated in Singapore).
- **Risks** the guidelines list include, among others, blood clots, raised prolactin and liver problems (transfeminine HRT), and a raised red blood cell count, liver problems and blood pressure (transmasculine HRT).

#### If surgery is considered
{: #h-minors-surgery}

Surgery is also exceptional between 18 and 20 and needs the TRP's agreement after a thorough assessment. Before surgery:

- you'll generally need **at least 12 months of HRT prescribed and monitored by your attending doctor**. Hormones you got yourself, or prescribed elsewhere, don't count towards this;
- you'll generally need to have **lived as your gender for at least 12 months**;
- **sex reassignment surgery** (removal of reproductive organs and genital surgery) also needs a review by a **Clinical Ethics Committee**;
- you, your parents (and any future spouse) should be referred for **counselling on reproductive options**, and you should be offered **financial counselling**.

Your surgeon should also explain how each procedure affects changing the gender marker on your NRIC or passport. 

### If you're taking hormones you got yourself
{: #h-minors-self-procured}

The guidelines say that if you tell a doctor you're taking hormones without a prescription, they should **assess whether you're suitable to be prescribed hormones** and give you medical advice about what you're taking. Doctors can't verify where self-sourced hormones came from or what's in them, and contamination or the wrong dose can cause serious side effects.

**<u>READ THIS BEFORE YOU BEGIN SELF-MANAGED HRT AS A MINOR</u>**

**In general - we do *not* approve of minors beginning self-managed HRT, especially if you are in a home that is not welcoming or approving of your transgender identity.**

Self-managed HRT refers to taking hormones without a prescription or doctor supervision. The import of prescription medications without a prescription is in breach of customs law.

Legality aside - the primary concern with doctors - even with adults - is your own safety in starting HRT. There are several dimensions to this:

- *Safe medication* - this means having a reputable source, understanding dosage rates, and how much you're supposed to take. More does not always mean better; more might mean that you may gain unwanted effects to your liver, kidneys and your heart, all of which need to be monitored, which mean costly blood tests that at your age <u>will be impossible to order without alerting your parents.</u>

  This also means understanding how it affects factors like fertility, as well as decisions that you may want to make in the future. You will not be able to bank gametes at your age.

  For transmasculine people - sources of T can be contaminated and suspicious, even if you get it from a trusted source, and testing can be costly and impossible.
- *Safe transition* - this also means having a plan with regard to the social aspects of your transition. What happens if parents or your school finds out? What happens if medical professionals find out and order a battery of tests? What do you say then in circumstances like that?

  You'll be putting yourself on a knife's edge without a plan in case things go horribly wrong. You'll also face potential disruptions in supply - *this will almost certainly happen for many reasons, including financial, familial and social*.
- *Psychological and emotional support* - this means that you need people around you who can answer questions about what you're feeling.

  If you're merely questioning, you need someone who can work you through the effects of HRT, because you may experience very different effects if your brain isn’t primed to work on E or T (depending on the sex hormone you’re taking for your transition. Effects of depersonalisation or derealisation may appear, and you may experience some level of dysphoria that comes with introducing another sex hormone to your system if it isn't right for you \[this also occurs if you end up on a wrong dose\].

  The community can help, but a professional will be better equipped.

The number of people taking HRT and regretting it is low but not zero. You may have seen people talk about detransitioners. While they're absolutely valid and it is and absolutely valid thing to decide to be or to present as your birth gender at the end of your gender journey, the notion that you may be experiencing some irreversible change will be psychologically heavy, especially if you aren't prepared or are not well-informed.

You'll also need that same support when for some reason you run out of supply, or worse, if your supply is discovered and your family decides to grill you upon their source. What will you say? It’s likely they will not allow you to take it if they are not supportive, by which time you’ll experience hormonal flux that will cause, amongst other things, thoughts of self-harm and suicide.

A professional (like a counsellor) would be more equipped to help you handle the psychological effects, but you will not have good access to one outside of an educational institution until you're 18. A school counsellor will have good reason to consider this self-harm and break confidentiality to your parents if you tell them about this.

<u>Do not put yourself in harm’s way, even if you feel that this is the right thing to do.</u>

### For parents
{: #h-minors-parents}

The guidelines see parents as a key part of their child's care, at every stage.

- **Both parents are usually interviewed** during assessment, about their observations, their own views on gender, and how decisions are made in the family.
- **If you're not yet accepting,** separate sessions may be offered to you: information about gender dysphoria, space to talk through your beliefs, and support with feelings like grief or loss. Joint sessions with your child can follow.
- **If you and your child (or you and your co-parent) disagree,** the team can help with conflict resolution and finding common ground.
- **Your consent is required** before any medical or surgical treatment for anyone under 21.

The guidelines are clear that support from family matters, whatever treatment your child does or doesn't have.

### What you can do in the meanwhile
{: #h-c604555e-9609-491c-8cb0-f49a92d179b9}

Psychological care and support for gender dysphoria will be difficult without parental permission or approval. Some services have a lower age limit - TransBefrienders’ befriending support service begins at 16.

If your parents can afford it and are open to them, you can perhaps ask to go to a LGBTQ-affirming counselling and therapy service, which we will list on the [psychological and emotional resource guide]({{site.baseurl}}/guide/psychological-emotional-resources/#h-0a7f10d5-5ed2-4265-ab40-1939a1ab61a9). Functionally, having one parent consent should be enough, though some other places will have stricter rules on consent.

#### Coming out to parents - I’ll Walk With You
{: #h-e183015c-b325-4084-807b-55901b8e9439}

*I’ll Walk With You* is a pretty comprehensive resource guide for parents of transgender children that is available in all four of Singapore’s official languages - Malay, English, Chinese and Tamil. It’s available in PDF form here: [https://www.congregaytion.com/ill-walk-with-you](https://www.congregaytion.com/ill-walk-with-you)

If you need to pick up a physical copy, you can do so at the Alicia Community Center - which is not open to the public. Ask on Transcend if there’ll be a meetup, or if you can obtain a physical copy from Transcend servermates who have access there.

#### Navigating school counselling systems
{: #h-e35a1f2f-db94-464b-a8a8-87f1a87db5b8}

The Ministry of Education counselling system lacks homogeneity overall, and it is not possible to guarantee that you will not be outed by your school counsellor or teacher if you opt to confide in them.

The rules and code of ethics and behavioural conduct by the Association of Psychotherapists and Counsellors of Singapore and the Singapore Association for Counselling state the confidentiality is *part of the foundation of trust between a client and a counsellor*. Confidentiality may only be broken when it is in a client’s interest; i.e. if a client were to express thoughts of self-harm and suicide.

Still, rules pertaining to disclosure with sexuality and gender remains something that depends by individual counsellors and their beliefs; they may talk about it with teachers, too, who are in turn <u><strong>not obligated to maintain confidentiality</strong></u>**.** We do not advise talking to school counsellors about queer issues — many, many, many of us have had negative experiences with them.

The Ministry of Education gives school counsellors a wide berth to exercise their professional judgement in breaking confidentiality with parents.

Hence — **be cautious** when it comes to discussing your gender identity and sexuality with your school counsellors.
