Self-managing transfeminine HRT can be a daunting and occasionally costly manoeuvre, especially when some components seem rather complicated and complex. Still, they are relatively easy to break down.

This document will be focused on talking about self-management of gender-affirming HRT.

This means, largely, that there will be little to no doctor supervision or approval expected. Still, doctor involvement - in blood testing - is highly recommended. If you do not have access to an endocrinologist (because of residency status or other reasons) it is recommended that you have a blood test to check your hormone and liver function levels once every four months.

**Read this before you start self-managed HRT as a minor.**

Self-managed HRT refers to taking hormones without a prescription or doctor supervision.

You should understand the various risks that you will undertake with self-managed HRT. There are several risks that you may undertake: 

- *Legal.* The import of prescription medication without a prescription is in breach of customs law. 

- *Safe medication* — this means having a reputable source, understanding dosage rates, and how much you're supposed to take. More does not always mean better; more might mean that you may gain unwanted effects to your liver, kidneys and your heart, all of which need to be monitored. This will mean costly blood tests that, if you're under 18, may be impossible to order without alerting your parents. 

	This also means understanding how HRT affects factors like fertility, as well as decisions that you may want to make in the future. You will not be able to bank gametes at your age.

	Sources of E and T can be contaminated and suspicious, even if you get it from a trusted source, and testing can be costly and impossible.
  
- *Safe transition* - this also means having a plan with regard to the social aspects of your transition. What happens if parents or your school finds out? What happens if you fall sick, and medical professionals discover a disparity in terms of your hormone levels in a blood test? What do you say then in circumstances like that?

	You'll be putting yourself on a knife's edge without a plan in case things go horribly wrong. You'll also face potential disruptions in supply - *this will almost certainly happen for many reasons, including financial, familial and social*.
  
- *Psychological and emotional risks* - you will need people around you who can answer questions about what you're feeling.

	If you're merely questioning, you need someone who can work you through the effects of HRT, because you may experience very different effects if your brain isn’t primed to work on E or T (depending on the sex hormone you’re taking for your transition. Effects of depersonalisation or derealisation may appear, and you may experience some level of dysphoria that comes with introducing another sex hormone to your system if it isn't right for you \[this also occurs if you end up on a wrong dose\]. *It is not recommended for you to take HRT if you are below 18 and are merely questioning.* 

	The community can help, but a professional will be better equipped.

- *Family risks* - you are probably dependent on your parents for shelter and financial support. If you live in a home that is not welcoming or approving, you may end up facing familial violence. 

The number of people taking HRT and regretting it is low but not zero. You may have seen people talk about detransitioners. While they're absolutely valid and it is and absolutely valid thing to decide to be or to present as your birth gender at the end of your gender journey, the notion that you may be experiencing some permanent change will be psychologically heavy, especially if you aren't prepared or are not well-informed.

You'll also need that same support when for some reason you run out of supply, or worse, if your supply is discovered and your family decides to grill you upon their source. What will you say? It’s likely they will not allow you to take it if they are not supportive, by which time you’ll experience hormonal flux that will cause, amongst other things, thoughts of self-harm and suicide. 

A professional (like a counsellor) would be more equipped to help you handle the psychological effects, but you will not have good access to one outside of an educational institution until you're 18. A school counsellor will have good reason to consider this self-harm and break confidentiality to your parents if you tell them about this.

There are many risks involved with family, too, given that you may be dependent on them for shelter and financial support. If you live in a home that is not welcoming or approving, you may face familial violence. 

<u>It is important for you to weigh these risks before you begin self-managed HRT.</u>

The guidelines say that if you do inform a doctor that you're taking hormones without a prescription, they should **assess whether you're suitable to be prescribed hormones** and give you medical advice about what you're taking. Contamination and inappropriate dosing regimens can lead to safety risks and serious side effects.

#### Blood testing

This is the most important portion of *safe* self-management. While it may be possible for you to safely consume estradiol and anti-androgens if you get them from a reputable source, you’ll need some knowledge of your own E and T levels before adjusting your dosage (and knowing the levels that are suitable for you).

*Getting blood tests*

There are several GP clinics where you can get your blood tested. You have to be at least above 18 for these tests; this is largely because of parental consent, as well as parents having access to your blood test data if you have yet to be of age or separated your healthcare data from your parents.

You can find a list of doctors in the private sector here: [🥼List of private doctors and general practitioners]({{site.baseurl}}/guide/private-doctors/)

#### Acquiring safe medication
{: #h-1b87d16e-1578-471f-9a0b-1fa4d09984eb}

There are several safe medication sources, all online. Since the demise of [hrt.cafe](http://hrt.cafe), several other sites have sprung up, all offering the same directories:

- [https://valerie.vg/](https://valerie.vg/)

*Before you make your purchase*

Be sure to browse where the source of your HRT will be from, and whether they will ship to Singapore. Some suppliers - like Favskinhouse and Amazing4Health - have been a proven source of E for many transfems here. InHousePharmacy requires that you show that you have a prescription, which may be difficult.

You’ll also need to decide at least on your own dosages. Take a look at dosage equivalence in this [Transfeminine Science article](https://transfemscience.org/articles/e2-equivalent-doses/); determining your dosages will help in determining supply. You should keep some record of your self-managed dosages that you can show to a doctor once you see one. A good starting dose will begin at the low end - 2mg sublingual estradiol hemihydrate and 12.5mg cyproterone acetate (or equivalent).

Both DIY directories state that DashPCT ships from Singapore, but its website states quite clearly that international pharmacy shipments originate in India; as of this moment we do not have grey market unofficial sources that ship from our country. *DashPCT does ship to Singapore.*

*When you make your purchase*

Ensure that you are personally safe and able to pay and receive for the package. This means that you should not be using a payment method that is tracked by someone else, and that you should not be using a reception method that is unsafe for you.

Most sources will have a discreet way of packaging your delivery. Some places do opt for the flamboyant packaging route (like OTNK) but those are often niche and will have an option for discreet packaging.

#### Safe transition
{: #h-be407c10-8074-4d25-9984-c6e8d7f938b5}

If you’re just starting out and you have access to a polyclinic or a gender clinic, we heavily recommend that you begin on the route of getting to a gender clinic as soon as you can. This is because the most reliable way to maintain and ensure supply for HRT is to go to a hospital or GP.

You should check out [the guide to gender-affirming care]({{site.baseurl}}/guide/gender-affirming-healthcare/) in this case.

Have a plan with regards to the social aspects of your transition, and understand these plans with regards to your school or workplace. Make plans to speak with the people closest to you that you’re certain will understand, and seek help on Transcend if you need.

**As with all transition-related processes, it is important to understand that you should make a plan should the people around you be unable to accept you or who you are.** Have plans of safety in mind. Trans health and trans social care can be and IS overstretched. You may not be able to access trans social care in the worst case scenarios.

<u>A note on injectables</u>

If you’re choosing to purchase an injectable from the above directories, note that the injectables have not been tested largely; understand that **you are doing so at your own risk**.

The Ukraine war means that Lena, who had reliably supplied injectable estradiol valerate to many around the world, is no longer doing so, causing a crop of homebrew injectable suppliers to appear around the world. This isn’t a necessarily terrible thing; other suppliers, such as Astrovials, OTNK and OE Labs, have been consistent and forward with sending their injectables with testing, but it means that the field of people who supply injectables have expanded and each of them harbour varying qualities of medication.

Do your homework before purchase; look at what others have said in their reviews, speak to the community, and read what the supplier says with regards to the source of their raws (the powder formulation that is mixed into a solvent to form an injectable).

An organisation focused on harm reduction has begun testing samples for their purity; you can take a look at their results [here](https://transharmreduction.org/hrt-testing). Still, they are largely based in Europe and the UK and may not have tested sources that are closer to home.

#### Frequently asked questions about self-managed HRT
{: #h-1dc48ba4-625c-4088-9f9b-3a1cfcd27c18}

1. <u>Will self-managed HRT affect your psychiatrist evaluations?</u>

   There is no evidence that self-managed HRT will affect your chances in being formally diagnosed with gender dysphoria. To some extent, if you are feeling comfort in transitioning, the psychiatrist can see this as definitive proof that you do indeed have GD.

   This isn’t to say that if you don’t take self-managed HRT you won’t be diagnosed. There are many different ways dysphoria can be evaluated.
2. <u>Can I experiment while self-managing HRT?</u>

   Irreversible changes to your body will not happen until more than 3 months into taking HRT, so experimentation is partly possible. Do understand, however, that you may experience some hormonal flux if you stop from a fairly high dose. This may lead to some adverse effects, like thoughts of self-harm, lethargy, amongst others.

   If you do experiment, it is important that you journal and record down your emotions and how you feel about yourself, and regularly seek some support between friends.
