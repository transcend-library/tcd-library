Gender affirming care for minors has been incredibly difficult in this country — and will continue to be, with Circular 44/2026 banning HRT for those under 18 and significantly narrowing the route to get HRT for those between 18 and 21.

A young trans person aged between 18 and 20 will now have to go through a new Treatment Review Panel, which will consist of at least four professionals — a psychiatrist, an endocrinologist, a counsellor (or social worker), and an additional non-treating medical specialist — after getting consent from both their parents and guardians. 

Trans youth below 18 are effectively banned from puberty blockers, hormone replacement therapy or surgical intervention. There is no workaround in the private sector, as the Circular applies to everyone in the medical sector.

## At a glance
{: #h-minors-at-a-glance}

| | Psychological support | Puberty blockers | Hormone therapy (HRT) | Surgery |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Before puberty** | Yes | No | No | No |
| **Puberty to 17** | Yes | No | No | No |
| **18 to 20** | Yes | No | After TRP approval with parents' consent | After TRP approval with parents consent |

<br>**Puberty blockers are not recommended at any age.** The Ministry of Health have cited studies claiming that there are not enough high-quality evidence on their safety or effectiveness, and puberty blockers are mostly useless when you're 18.  

For most, the guidelines expect you to only be able to make decisions or consent only after you're 21 — as that's the legal age of majority in Singapore.

**Read this before you start self-managed HRT as a minor.**

Self-managed HRT refers to taking hormones without a prescription or doctor supervision.

You should understand the various risks that you will undertake with self-managed HRT. There are several risks that you may undertake: 

- *Legal.* The import of prescription medication without a prescription is in breach of customs law. 

- *Safe medication* — this means having a reputable source, understanding dosage rates, and how much you're supposed to take. More does not always mean better; more might mean that you may gain unwanted effects to your liver, kidneys and your heart, all of which need to be monitored. This will mean costly blood tests that, if you're under 18, may be impossible to order without alerting your parents. 

	This also means understanding how HRT affects factors like fertility, as well as decisions that you may want to make in the future. You will not be able to bank gametes at your age.

	Sources of E and T can be contaminated and suspicious, even if you get it from a trusted source, and testing can be costly and impossible.
  
- *Safe transition* - this also means having a plan with regard to the social aspects of your transition. What happens if parents or your school finds out? What happens if you fall sick, and medical professionals discover a disparity in terms of your hormone levels in a blood test? What do you say then in circumstances like that?

	You'll be putting yourself on a knife's edge without a plan in case things go horribly wrong. You'll also face potential disruptions in supply - *this will almost certainly happen for many reasons, including financial, familial and social*.
  
- *Psychological and emotional risks* - you will need people around you who can answer questions about what you're feeling.

	If you're merely questioning, you need someone who can work you through the effects of HRT, because you may experience very different effects if your brain isn’t primed to work on E or T (depending on the sex hormone you’re taking for your transition. Effects of depersonalisation or derealisation may appear, and you may experience some level of dysphoria that comes with introducing another sex hormone to your system if it isn't right for you \[this also occurs if you end up on a wrong dose\]. *It is not recommended for you to take HRT if you are below 18 and are merely questioning.* 

	The community can help, but a professional will be better equipped.

- *Family risks* - you are probably dependent on your parents for shelter and financial support. If you live in a home that is not welcoming or approving, you may end up facing familial violence. 

The number of people taking HRT and regretting it is low but not zero. You may have seen people talk about detransitioners. While they're absolutely valid and it is and absolutely valid thing to decide to be or to present as your birth gender at the end of your gender journey, the notion that you may be experiencing some permanent change will be psychologically heavy, especially if you aren't prepared or are not well-informed.

You'll also need that same support when for some reason you run out of supply, or worse, if your supply is discovered and your family decides to grill you upon their source. What will you say? It’s likely they will not allow you to take it if they are not supportive, by which time you’ll experience hormonal flux that will cause, amongst other things, thoughts of self-harm and suicide. 

A professional (like a counsellor) would be more equipped to help you handle the psychological effects, but you will not have good access to one outside of an educational institution until you're 18. A school counsellor will have good reason to consider this self-harm and break confidentiality to your parents if you tell them about this.

There are many risks involved with family, too, given that you may be dependent on them for shelter and financial support. If you live in a home that is not welcoming or approving, you may face familial violence. 

<u>It is important for you to weigh these risks before you begin self-managed HRT.</u>

The guidelines say that if you do inform a doctor that you're taking hormones without a prescription, they should **assess whether you're suitable to be prescribed hormones** and give you medical advice about what you're taking. Contamination and inappropriate dosing regimens can lead to safety risks and serious side effects.

## What you can get at any age
{: #h-minors-any-age}

- **Assessment and diagnosis** of gender dysphoria
- **Psychological support:** individual therapy, family sessions, and group therapy. *The goals of any therapy should be explained to you, and you should agree to it; the guidelines say therapy without your consent and involvement will not be recommended.*
- **Help with other things you're going through,** like anxiety, depression, bullying, stigma, school stress or friendships.
- **Support for your parents,** including separate sessions if they're not yet accepting.
- **Help with school.** The guidelines say accommodations at school (and other organisations) should be worked out together between you, your family and the school.
- **Information** about your options, what to expect, and effects on fertility.

It should be noted that even though these are what the guidelines say you can expect, you may not be able to get assistance with some of these items — especially if they hinder your school work, exam performance, etc. Each person's experience with these things will also vary, and will be very dependent on whether the people you meet are approving, accommodating, or understand that you are struggling as a young person. Still — hang in there! 

## What is the Treatment Review Panel?
{: #h-minors-trp}

The Treatment Review Panel (RTRP is a panel of professionals who decide, by consensus, whether hormones or surgery should be part of your treatment plan. At minimum, it will include:

- your psychiatrist;
- the psychologists, counsellors or social workers involved in your care;
- your endocrinologist or paediatrician;
- a surgeon, if surgery is being considered; and
- one more specialist who isn't involved in your treatment.

If you'll need an adult endocrinologist, one will be on the panel. (In some clusters, an adult endo may see you if you're between 18 to 20 either way.)

**You and both parents should be invited into the meeting with the TRP.** If HRT is agreed upon, your endocrinologist or paediatrician will inform you of it; if surgery is agreed upon, your surgeon will inform you of that. 

The panel will look at things like why you want to transition medically, what might happen to your mental health if you don't, whether it may reduce your dysphoria, timing around milestones like **National Service, school or work**, and how accepting the people around you are likely to be.

They will also check that you're able to make the decision (your **capacity**). This means showing that you:

- understand gender dysphoria and how it affects your life;
- understand the treatment, including realistic outcomes, which changes are permanent, and what it means for fertility;
- can weigh up the risks and benefits;
- are not under pressure from other people or information, in person or online; and
- do not have a mental health condition that's affecting your decision-making.

Things to know about the TRP:

- **The TRP's decision isn't binding on other doctors.** If one panel agrees to HRT and you later see a different endocrinologist, they don't have to prescribe the same treatment.
- **How meetings are run and charged will vary between doctors and hospitals.** If cost is a concern, you should ask about this up front. 
- **Treatment continues to be reviewed.** If you start HRT (or have surgery), your condition, and whether you and/or your parents are still willing to continue treatment, will be reviewed regularly during your transition. Any major change goes back to the panel, which reviews whether to continue.

## How do you get to the Treatment Review Panel? 
{: #h-TRP-route}

Starting the assessment for the treatment review panel involves getting referrals for specialist outpatient clinics (SOC). This usually begins at a polyclinic, or can begin if you are at, say, the IMH Child Guidance Clinic or seeing a psychiatrist at a public hospital.

| Catchment areas ([*what are catchment areas?*](guide/gender-affirming-healthcare/catchment-areas/)) | Between 18 to 20 years old | Below 18 years old |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **NHG Health** | IMH SOC | IMH SOC |
| **NUHS** | NUH Psychological Medicine | NUH Psychological Medicine |
| **SingHealth** | CGH Psychiatric Medicine SOC, SGH Psychiatric Medicine SOC, SKH Psychiatric Medicine SOC | KKH Adolescent Medicine SOC |

<br>The SOCs are where your assessments are expected to begin. None of the TRP pathways have been set up yet (as of writing {% include relative-time.html date="2026-10-05" prefix="on " %}). so this guide will focus on what will be expected. 

### Getting assessed
{: #h-22b2690f-21a7-4913-a25b-c63c59555b28}

Assessments will be thorough and can take a long time. Clinicians were advised in the guidelines to "take the necessary time to gather relevant information and perform careful assessments" before making a gender dysphoria diagnosis. While it is not standardised or formally recommended, MOH cites published data that suggests an assessment period of 12-18 months.

In public hospitals, you will likely be assessed in a team. If you are seeing a psychiatrist at a private clinic, you may be referred to other private medical professionals. 

You should expect to talk about: 

- how you've felt about your gender over time, including in childhood, and how puberty has affected that;
- your family, school and social life, and any difficult experiences;
- any social transition you've done so far, and how people reacted.

*Both of your parents will be interviewed.* They will be asked about what they've observed, their views on gender, and how decisions are usually made in your family. If your parents disagree with each other, the team will do a further family assessment before reaching a diagnosis or making recommendations. 

The team will also check for other conditions that can overlap with gender dysphoria or affect how it's understood, such as autism, depression, anxiety, PTSD, eating disorders or body dysmorphia. Any other condition should be treated too. You may be also asked to fill in questionnaires.

Understand that medical professionals generally see cisness as the default. If you prepare beforehand as to how to answer some questions, they may see this as evidence of influence — that you may have been guided towards how to answer questions, and that you may have been told as to what to do. 

Psychiatrists may also quiz you on your non-binary gender identity if you are non-binary. They may not understand what non-binary gender identities are. 

At the end of the assessment, you may obtain a diagnosis. This does not automatically mean medical treatment, however — the new guidelines state that treatment plans should be individual and assess you holistically. 

### For children (before puberty)
{: #h-minors-before-puberty}

MOH defines children as "a person in the developmental period between infancy and puberty". The children's guidelines apply only to children who have not yet reached puberty, meaning before Tanner stage 2. 

The guidelines describe puberty as when a child develops secondary sexual characteristics and reproductive function. They give the first clinical signs as:

- breast budding (breast Tanner stage 2) in girls;
- testicular enlargement in boys.

Once puberty begins (Tanner stage 2), a young person counts as an adolescent under the separate adolescent guidelines, until they turn 21.

No medical treatment is given before puberty. Support will only be psychological and family-based. Assessment for children will involve joint sessions with the child and parents, over **at least 6 months to a year**.

On social transition — families are encouraged to talk openly about it; where parents are against it, professionals may help them find a middle ground (for example, in certain situations only).

The guidelines will also ask professionals to support parents in letting the child's gender identity develop freely, including returning to one that matches their sex assigned at birth.

As the child approaches puberty, care should move smoothly to adolescent services.

### Obtaining gender-affirming care when you're between 18 and 20
{: #h-4d93e0bf-6ffb-4d23-9c96-0be896ea0874}

Hormones or surgery can only be offered if **all** of these conditions are met: 

1. You are **over 18**.
2. There is **clear evidence of benefit or harm reduction**, which may include medical or mental health grounds.
3. A **Treatment Review Panel (TRP)** has agreed.
4. **You and your parents** have given signed, informed consent.

"Clear evidence of benefit or harm reduction" is not a fixed criteria. You will be assessed individually, with your wellbeing and safety as the primary consideration. 

#### Consent from your parents
{: #h-minors-consent}

Signed informed consent is needed from **you and your parents** before any treatment starts.

- If one parent **can't be contacted** (for example, they live apart from you or refuse contact), consent from one parent is accepted, as long as a reasonable effort was made to reach the other.
- If neither parent can be contacted, your **legal guardian** can consent.

#### If HRT is agreed
{: #h-minors-hrt}

- HRT is started and managed by an **endocrinologist**, only after puberty and from age 18.
- **Before starting,** expect a full check-up (medical, family and medication history, a physical exam) and blood tests: blood count, kidney and liver function, hormone levels, prolactin (for transfeminine HRT), cholesterol and blood sugar, calcium and vitamin D, plus a bone density (DXA) scan and possibly a bone age X-ray.
- **Doses start low and are increased gradually,** to mimic a natural puberty.
  - *Transfeminine HRT:* estradiol (tablets or patches), with an anti-androgen (spironolactone or cyproterone) or a GnRH agonist to lower testosterone.
  - *Transmasculine HRT:* testosterone injections every two weeks.
- **Monitoring** until second puberty is complete: height, weight and blood pressure every 3 months; hormone levels and vitamin D every 6 months (plus prolactin for transfem HRT, and haematocrit and cholesterol for transmasc HRT); and a bone density scan every year. After that, monitoring is the same as for adults.
- **Fertility** must be discussed with you and both parents before starting, including the chance that fertility is affected long-term, and options to preserve it (such as sperm banking or egg freezing, which are regulated in Singapore).
- **Risks** the guidelines list include, among others, blood clots, raised prolactin and liver problems (transfeminine HRT), and a raised red blood cell count, liver problems and blood pressure (transmasculine HRT).

#### If surgery is considered
{: #h-minors-surgery}

Surgery is also exceptional between 18 and 20 and needs the TRP's agreement after a thorough assessment. Before surgery:

- you'll generally need **at least 12 months of HRT prescribed and monitored by your attending doctor**. Hormones you got yourself, or prescribed elsewhere will not count towards this;
- you'll generally need to have **lived as your gender for at least 12 months**;
- **sex reassignment surgery** (removal of reproductive organs and genital surgery) also needs a review by a **Clinical Ethics Committee**;
- you, your parents (and any future spouse) should be referred for **counselling on reproductive options**, and you should be offered **financial counselling**. 

The guidelines warn that cheaper surgery overseas may come with too little counselling and a higher risk of complications or regret.

It's fairly unlikely, if you're between 18 to 20, for you to get surgery scheduled before you turn 21. Even if, say, you begin your assessment right on your 18th birthday, you'll still need to have had at least 12 months of HRT prescribed before you are eligible for surgery — i.e. you'll only be eligible when you turn 20, and from there you only have another 12 months before you turn 21 and can be assessed for surgery through the adult route. 

### What you can do in the meanwhile
{: #h-c604555e-9609-491c-8cb0-f49a92d179b9}

Psychological care and support for gender dysphoria will be difficult without parental permission or approval. Some services have a lower age limit - TransBefrienders’ befriending support service begins at 16.

If your parents can afford it and are open to them, you can perhaps ask to go to a LGBTQ-affirming counselling and therapy service, which we will list on the [psychological and emotional resource guide]({{site.baseurl}}/guide/psychological-emotional-resources/#h-0a7f10d5-5ed2-4265-ab40-1939a1ab61a9). Functionally, having one parent consent should be enough, though some other places will have stricter rules on consent.

#### Coming out to parents - I’ll Walk With You
{: #h-e183015c-b325-4084-807b-55901b8e9439}

*I’ll Walk With You* is a pretty comprehensive resource guide for parents of transgender children that is available in all four of Singapore’s official languages - Malay, English, Chinese and Tamil. It’s available in PDF form here: [https://www.congregaytion.com/ill-walk-with-you](https://www.congregaytion.com/ill-walk-with-you)

Support from parents is a key part of obtaining gender-affirming care when you are between 18 and 21. It's important to note that there are support *for* parents out there, including with *My Family Matters*, a safe space for parents of queer and trans children to speak with others, run by TransgenderSG and Oogachaga. Follow [TransSG's Instagram](https://www.instagram.com/transgendersg) for more details.

#### Navigating the school counselling systems
{: #h-e35a1f2f-db94-464b-a8a8-87f1a87db5b8}

The Ministry of Education counselling system lacks homogeneity overall, and it is not possible to guarantee that you will not be outed by your school counsellor or teacher if you opt to confide in them.

The rules and code of ethics and behavioural conduct by the Association of Psychotherapists and Counsellors of Singapore and the Singapore Association for Counselling state the confidentiality is *part of the foundation of trust between a client and a counsellor*. Confidentiality may only be broken when it is in a client’s interest; i.e. if a client were to express thoughts of self-harm and suicide.

Still, rules pertaining to disclosure with sexuality and gender remains something that depends by individual counsellors and their beliefs; they may talk about it with teachers, too, who are in turn <u><strong>not obligated to maintain confidentiality</strong></u>**.** 

We do not advise talking to school counsellors about queer issues — many, many, many of us have had negative experiences with them. 

While recent changes to counselling guidelines have meant that the risk of being outed has been lowered, school counsellors continue to have a wide berth to exercise their professional judgement in breaking confidentiality with parents. 

Hence — **be cautious** when it comes to discussing your gender identity and sexuality with your school counsellors.
