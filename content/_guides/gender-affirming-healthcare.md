## Introduction
{: #h-844fa814-572d-4c22-a675-3eddd850a1a4}

If you're reading this, you're probably starting or intending to start hormone replacement therapy through the public healthcare system, and wondering how to start. 

When this guide was first written in 2022, we were faced with a healthcare system in flux. The closure of IMH's Gender Care Clinic created a lot of confusion at the time, with many differing experiences on where and when the process began, setting so many things in question. 

Circular 44/2026 is expected to do the same those who are under 21. It's already made things difficult for many between 18 and 21, creating a de-facto ban for gender-affirming hormone therapy in that age range (unless, you're somehow blessed with two parents who would consent to you being transgender). And even with psychological support available at any age, it will still be hard to understand how your body is changing against your own will and deal with that. 

It's important to understand that the healthcare system is fraught for us. Not everyone will be an ally. You may not feel safe all the time; in fact there may be times where it doesn’t feel safe at all, with the nurse obliged to call out your legal name; with your legal gender listed in your hospital records. 

The hope with this document is that we can help you cut through the noise, and understand what to expect. It is a living document and there may be gaps and holes that we will try to patch for most use cases and scenarios.

If you’ve joined a community group, you’ve heard names being mentioned. To protect the people who are serving our community, we are keeping most of their names (or their full names) out of this document.

With the closure of the adult Gender Care Clinic at the Institute of Mental Health, several routes have opened up for trans people to obtain gender-affirming care. Gender-affirming healthcare consists of two different components: endocrinology and psychological medicine (an umbrella term that consists of both psychiatrists and psychologists).

Gender-affirming healthcare for minors is technically unavailable in the public healthcare sector, but workarounds exist primarily in the private healthcare sector.

## A summary of the public and private routes to gender-affirming healthcare
{: #h-ec695659-d1f4-47a2-bc69-0793215adae1}

Gender-affirming healthcare - especially for medical transition - has two separate components: **endocrinology** and **psychological medicine** (psychologists and psychiatrists).

Endocrinologists assess you for medical health risks with regard to transition, prescribe and manage your hormone doses, and follow up with potential health risks related to transition; psychologists and psychiatrists assess you for gender dysphoria (per the medical definition) and are also available to assist with social and psychological aspects of transition.

### The basic process
{: #h-5b9c47db-b097-46fd-9edb-d86de3600311}

If you are **21 or above** and are intending to transition, or wish to have a formal memo or evaluation for gender dysphoria through the public health system, your first step is to go to the polyclinic.

**If you are under 21**, read the [*gender-affirming care for minors and young adults*]({{site.baseurl}}/guide/gender-affirming-healthcare/below-21/) guide.

This guide presumes that you are above 21.  

#### **Being referred from the polyclinic**
{: #h-1bd43ae6-c2ce-42ab-8c76-5bc57f5fdea9}

Your experience will vary depending on the general practitioner that you will see, but most GPs will understand and accept that you wish to have a referral to either hospital (NUH or CGH) for gender-related issues. The GPs understand that this is more or less a job for a specialist.

*Ensure that the point of contact that you have with the polyclinic is a contact number you have control of.* Verify this with the nurse at the counter when you first visit, to ensure that your appointments are not sent to someone else by mistake. Even if you're above 21, your contact may be that or your parent or guardian. Have it changed if this is the case. 

Most polyclinics are able to make a referral to either NUH, KTPH, TTSH or CGH if you specifically request for them. There are three general options - National University Hospital, Changi General Hospital, Tan Tock Seng Hospital, or Khoo Teck Puat Hospital, depending on your catchment area. 

Read: [What are catchment areas?]({{site.baseurl}}/guide/gender-affirming-healthcare/catchment-areas/)

<div class="indented" markdown="1">

**For NUH - referrals can generally be obtained in polyclinics under the [National University Polyclinic](https://www.nup.com.sg/) system.** For those living under the catchment area, the first stop will be NUH Psychological Medicine. If you live outside the catchment area, you can try and get a referral to the NUH Endocrinology department, but this may be tricky.

We have had at least one instance of someone being rejected for not living under the catchment area, though this may be happening because doctors don’t have a good understanding of how the different clusters work. \[Cross-cluster referrals happen and are allowed.\]

**For CGH - referrals can be obtained at most polyclinics in the east, especially those under the [SingHealth](https://polyclinic.singhealth.com.sg/) system. You may be rejected for living outside the catchment area, or transferred to NUH instead.**

**For KTPH** - referrals can largely be obtained from polyclinics within the National Healthcare Group cluster. Not much is known about this route, except that they have psychiatrists who can evaluate and approve you for HRT. You may also be referred to TTSH — which has an established endocrinology department — after an evaluation at KTPH or IMH. 

</div>

*Specify where you wish to go.* Some GPs may not have the knowledge, or may send you to IMH instead, where the Gender Care Clinic has closed since early 2022. Be careful to not insist upon the specific specialist who you want to see, as you will not be eligible for subsidies if you do so.

### Accessing gender-affirming HRT across all known routes
{: #h-830fc323-de70-4410-bfd6-a653a36b81e3}

<aside class="callout callout-pink">
<div class="callout-icon" aria-hidden="true">⚠️</div>
<div class="callout-body" markdown="1">

If you are self-managing HRT and do not have approval from a psychologist or endocrinologist (either from the local healthcare system or gender clinics overseas), you will not be able to obtain testing for hormone levels from the public health system.

If you require a blood test, it is best if you can do so through a GP. A list of private GPs can be found [here.]({{site.baseurl}}/guide/private-doctors/)

</div>
</aside>

The pathway is long and winding for some and more direct for others, and varying factors mean that wait time to approval and access differ individual by individual. Across the main clinics offering gender-affirming care for adults (>21), the first stop may vary — a psychiatrist, or a endocrinologist. 

For anyone under 21, MOH Circular 44/2026 (5 May 2026) now sets the rules for **all** doctors, both public and private. 

<aside class="callout callout-purple">
<div class="callout-icon" aria-hidden="true"><img src="/assets/notion/gender-affirming-healthcare/783548188232515807.png" alt=""></div>
<div class="callout-body" markdown="1">

**What does MOH Circular 44/2026 say?**

On 5 May 2026, MOH issued treatment guidelines for children and adolescents with gender dysphoria. They apply to **all registered doctors, in both public and private healthcare**. Doctors who don't follow them can be referred to the Singapore Medical Council.

- **Under 18:** hormonal and surgical treatment should **not** be offered, even with both parents' consent.
- **18 to 20:** hormones (or surgery) can be offered **only in exceptional circumstances**, where there is clear evidence of benefit or harm reduction (which may include medical or mental health grounds). It also needs:
  - **Prior review and agreement by a Treatment Review Panel (TRP):** a psychiatrist, the psychologists, counsellors or social workers involved in your care, an endocrinologist or paediatrician, a surgeon if surgery is being considered, and one doctor who isn't involved in your treatment.
  - **Written informed consent from you *and* both parents:** one parent is enough only if the other can't be contacted; a legal guardian if neither parent is available.
  - **Regular TRP reviews** for as long as treatment continues.
- **Puberty blockers are not offered at any age under 21.** If treatment is approved for an 18–20-year-old, you'll only get gender-affirming hormones.

</div>
</aside>

#### **The CGH route**
{: #h-89e707a0-1b2b-47a0-8204-0a211323231c}

The CGH route begins through a polyclinic referral. The route with CGH will start with an assessment with the endocrinologist; you can also choose to obtain a referral to the psychological medicine department to speed things up. 

It is recommended that if you are undergoing HRT (either via self-administered or via an informed consent private health route) that you should ask for a referral to the endocrinologist, and inform the endo that you are undergoing HRT. 

If you are above 21, assessment by a psychologist may be made optional. You may be referred to one if you have expressed (or are evaluated to have) a need for psychological support. You will still need approval by a psychiatrist before you can obtain HRT.

#### **The NUH route**
{: #h-0d3633fb-7d1f-45ca-8400-89c2b680ac03}

<aside class="callout callout-blue">
<div class="callout-icon" aria-hidden="true">📒</div>
<div class="callout-body" markdown="1">

We’re hearing reports from servermates who have said that they have been rejected from a referral from the NUH Gender Clinic because they are out of their [catchment area]({{site.baseurl}}/guide/gender-affirming-healthcare/catchment-areas/) (i.e. don’t live in the west).

This may be happening if doctors are particularly inexperienced and do not understand how these referrals may work.

If this happens to you, you may have to try to obtain the referral from another polyclinic within the NUP cluster. See [*What are catchment areas?*]({{site.baseurl}}/guide/gender-affirming-healthcare/catchment-areas/) for more details.

</div>
</aside>

A key difference between the CGH route and the NUH route is the possibility for you to be referred to NUH's Psychological Medicine specialist outpatient clinic as a first stop. This may happen especially if you live within the [catchment area]({{site.baseurl}}/guide/gender-affirming-healthcare/catchment-areas/).

If you live outside the catchment area, you can opt to be referred to the NUH endocrinologist.

While NUH can evaluate you with their own psychiatrist at the Gender Care Clinic, **a psychiatrist memo for gender dysphoria from a private psychiatrist may also be accepted.**

#### The route *from* IMH
{: #h-0dd2d765-fa85-4594-a93d-fe8b756f174c}

If you are above 21 and are regularly seeing a general psychiatrist at the Institute for Mental Health, it is possible to obtain a referral directly to either NUH or CGH. IMH is aware that they have shut the Gender Care Clinic and that they won’t be able to evaluate you for gender dysphoria.

If you see a psychotherapist assigned to you at IMH’s Clinic B/NAMS, you may have to get referred to a psychiatrist. Psychotherapists will not have the power to do a referral, but can add to a referral made by a psychiatrist with notes.

You’ll be able to be referred to an endocrinologist at CGH or NUH and begin accessing gender-affirming HRT from there. It should be likely that you can request for a specific clinic.

If you are not seeing an IMH psychiatrist, you should <u><strong>NOT</strong></u> get yourself referred to IMH Clinic B/NAMS; in effect getting a referral to IMH only to be referred out to a gender clinic is an unnecessary step here. This step is only recommended if you have been regularly seeing an IMH psychiatrist for other issues, if you were at the Child Guidance Clinic for other issues, or want to seek help at IMH for other mental health conditions. IMH’s general psychiatrists may not have a good understanding of gender dysphoria and are less likely to be able to give you a formal diagnosis.

If you are between the ages of 18 to 21, and are going the local polyclinic to start the Treatment Review Panel process you may be referred to an IMH Specialist Outpatient Clinic. You may be triaged to the appropriate subspecialty there, before being sent to TTSH or another hospital. 

#### Other places to get gender-affirming care
{: #h-3733bd9a-13d8-4cf0-88b5-d17167831e8d}

<aside class="callout callout-purple">
<div class="callout-icon" aria-hidden="true">❤️‍🩹</div>
<div class="callout-body" markdown="1">

The Ministry of Health is appearing to push for gender-affirming hormone therapy to be available across all hospitals, so the list below is likely to increase in the coming year.

It is also likely that this would mean that obtaining gender-affirming hormone therapy would be simplified for anyone who is above 21.

</div>
</aside>

There are several other hospitals that also provide gender-affirming hormone therapy, though they do not provide their own diagnostic approvals. These approvals would have to come from a private psychiatrist or a psychiatrist from another public hospital.

The Institute of Mental Health (through a general psychiatrist) may be able to give you a formal diagnosis, but this is fairly unlikely and their referrals to endocrinology departments may be rejected. When that happens, it is possible for you to attempt to be referred to an endocrinology department in these hospitals through a polyclinic consultation.

These hospitals include:

<div class="indented" markdown="1">

- SGH - Singapore General Hospital (which also sees patients for gender-affirming surgery)
- TTSH - Tan Tock Seng Hospital (the primary endocrinology clinic that IMH's Gender Care Clinic used to refer people to, before it closed in 2022) 
- KTPH - Khoo Teck Puat Hospital (KTPH has a psychiatrist that can evaluate for gender dysphoria as well as an experienced endocrinologist)

</div>

**You can obtain a gender dysphoria diagnosis through the private system; it should count in getting diagnostic approval for HRT.** This diagnosis can be obtained through private evaluations by a private psychiatrist, who can write referrals for you. **Do note that some of these hospitals may also require an in-house evaluation before you start HRT.**

It is also possible for you to be referred to another hospital once you have been evaluated for gender dysphoria - especially if, say, the hospital where you first obtained your gender dysphoria evaluation is too far from where you live.

Assessments for gender dysphoria through the public system (and hence approval for HRT) are only reliably available through Changi General Hospital and National University Hospital.

#### Private routes
{: #h-1e3b5afb-fdb1-4aff-8be4-3a0c4400b94d}

Several private psychiatrists can evaluate you for gender dysphoria. Some private psychiatrists may be able to prescribe HRT on the first visit, though that may be costly; others might only be able to refer you to a private endocrinologist, or write you a referral that you can bring to a public health endocrinologist (see [here](#h-3733bd9a-13d8-4cf0-88b5-d17167831e8d)).

Private endocrinologists are typically more expensive, but may be advisable if subsidies do not apply to you (for instance, if you are not a citizen or resident here).

## What gender-affirming healthcare can do for you
{: #h-71299346-da57-4a4a-af7f-f470cc6d0272}

As it says on the tin, clearly - gender-affirming healthcare helps *affirm your gender*. That varies a lot, however, depending on how you feel about yourself and what you want to be; hence this section.

The primary objective of getting through the healthcare system is gender-affirming hormone therapy (or HRT for short). Still, assessments from psychiatrists and psychologists have been known to provide some assistance with social transition in the workplace, school or with family.

#### What the public healthcare system can do in self-reflection and discovery
{: #h-ee6866cc-4c2d-4ae1-84bc-1d5fc6ff9d7e}

While a majority of us go through the healthcare system certain that we are transgender, a significant minority enter the system still discovering gender dysphoria and still finding the answers towards how they feel about themselves and their gender.

If this describes why you are obtaining gender-affirming healthcare, a major suggestion we would make here would be to seek counselling in a space that will help give you the tools to understand yourself further.

If you’re hoping to socially transition without medical transition, counselling might also be a good help.

It is likely that the public healthcare system is not as equipped in assisting you in understanding your gender. Some hospitals - like CGH - have trans-affirming psychologists, while other hospitals may not have psychologists who understand trans identity and the structural struggles we face.

We’ll expand more on this in the [psychological and emotional resource guide.]({{site.baseurl}}/guide/psychological-emotional-resources/#h-db96e90e-7dbc-42e5-9dc6-35e928e457a2)

#### What the public healthcare system can do in social transition
{: #h-bcd3ed2c-efef-489f-b0e8-45fb2f04222e}

Assessments from psychologists and psychiatrists can assist in providing evidence that you intend to transition if you are liable for National Service.

Specifically, every shred of proof that you have in your intention to socially transition to become anything but a cis man will help in making your case when you head for a medical review with the SAF, SCDF or SPF; from receipts for formal speech therapy (voice training) sessions to receipts for HRT, every item counts.

Take a look at the [transfem guide to National Service]({{site.baseurl}}/guide/national-service/) for more details.

The very same assessments can also help in acquiring certain accommodations from tertiary education institutions, though it may be difficult to change certain things (like names) without a deed poll. This will differ between institutions.

Additionally, through a joint session with a psychiatrist, it may be possible for you to help a loved one or relative understand the various aspects or reasons *why* you are transgender.

#### What gender-affirming hormone therapy (or HRT) can do for you
{: #h-f084d1e4-757e-4e3d-8802-4c9f53bc85be}

The biggest and most immediate relief, perhaps, that HRT can do for trans people is to alleviate dysphoria, an experience that is more common than even most trans people think.

With HRT, you’ll experience the bodily changes consistent with a desired puberty; with these changes you may be able to begin social transition with a body shape and other aspects that is closer to what you want. Still, these changes are not immediate and your mileage will certainly vary - this is a highly individualised process.

Have an understanding and desire on how you want to look as you progress through medical transition. While each person’s mileage from HRT will vary wildly, bodily changes will make it easier for you to present as your desired gender. Different factors, however - like familial support, relationships and workplace and educational discrimination might make it difficult for you to achieve certain aspects of medical transition, and <u>hence it is not advisable</u> for you to set a definite goal.

Medical transition isn’t necessary for certain aspects of determining your gender expression; you can, for instance, choose haircuts that frame your face in a more feminine or masculine way, use glasses that seem more gender-affirming, and opt to dress in a way that makes you look more like the gender you identify. It is possible that medical transition might make this easier; facial fat redistribution (in feminising HRT) and facial hair growth (in masculinizing HRT) will add to your gender expression and how you can be seen by others.

Be prepared, however, to make a social transition plan once you begin HRT; while social transition is not necessary with HRT, and both items can be entirely independent of each other, the way your appearance will change may engender the occasional intrusive question (from clueless cis people). This may be a source of anxiety as one experiences chest growth (for people taking feminizing hormone therapy) or voice changes and facial hair growth (for people taking masculinizing hormone therapy).

You can take a look at the [HRT guide for transmascs]({{site.baseurl}}/guide/transmasculine-hrt/) and [HRT guide for transfems]({{site.baseurl}}/guide/transfeminine-hrt/) for more information.

At the moment, it seems that non-binary medical transition - or, perhaps, medical transition that is specifically catered for non-binary goals - may seem more difficult to obtain than transmasculine or transfeminine hormone therapy in itself. You can take a look at the [transition guide for non-binary people]({{site.baseurl}}/guide/non-binary-transition/) for more information.

## Components of gender-affirming healthcare
{: #h-452d3b66-1864-49d3-9307-5057389eb782}

As we have previously established, gender-affirming healthcare chiefly involves endocrinology and psychological medicine.

Many of us will meet with psychiatrists only a handful of times in our journey after the initial diagnosis is given to you; meeting with a public health psychologist may also be helpful. Counselling is also another resource that you can depend on, if needed. But psychological medicine is something that we are less likely to encounter, compared to endocrinology.

For a safe medical transition, you’ll need to see the endocrinologist or do bloodwork at least three times a year at the start - once every four months. (Later in your transition, this will extend to once every six months, and then once a year.) 

In the Singapore system, an endocrinologist is where you also obtain your HRT (be in through gel, pills or patches). At NUH and CGH, the gender clinic is at least partially headed by an endocrinologist.

### Endocrinology
{: #h-263bd216-18bd-4a05-b399-daa510f95a58}

An endocrinologist’s main job, amongst others - is to ensure that HRT is not straining your liver or kidneys, and mitigate possible risks. They are also responsible for making sure that the pace of your second puberty is at a level that you want.

While some endocrinologists are in favour of some degree of experimentation whenever possible, others may not be. It is after all a personal choice, as long as you feel comfortable. To quote [Mia Mulder](https://youtu.be/rT4WjLWRRrw?t=4848): “Do what makes you happy, stop doing what doesn’t!”

Still though, there are limits to how much you can take. In most cases, it is not advisable to take more than the prescribed amount of HRT that you have been given, as the effects of a high dose can be damaging and long-lasting.

For instance, it would be unwise to take more than 50mg of cyproterone acetate (an anti-androgen) daily (though some endocrinologists will prescribe this initially); a major side effect of CPA overuse is elevated prolactin levels and the potential development of a prolactinoma.

Testosterone (T) levels for transmasculine HRT should ideally be between 5 nmol/L and 36 nmol/L, though levels of T that are good for one person may be dangerously high or low for another; if T levels are in the normal range, but hematocrit levels are too high, you may need a lower dosage (which is why blood tests are important; we go deeper into blood tests in the [transmasculine guide to HRT]({{site.baseurl}}/guide/transmasculine-hrt/#h-189a9997-ca49-480f-9ecc-86b82e0b502c)).

#### What to expect at your first visit with the endocrinologist
{: #h-5a9dd8f5-d317-49be-8a14-b1312e3528f7}

When you first visit the endocrinologist, they will do a preliminary evaluation for your gender dysphoria and related health risks for HRT from family medical histories. You do not need detailed documentation at this stage; though if you have them - like private psychiatrist memos, overseas dysphoria evaluations, logs kept during self-managed HRT - it will be good to produce them.

They will refer you to a psychiatrist if you haven't been referred to one. Depending on their evaluation, you may also be referred to a psychologist. At CGH, a panel consisting of the endocrinologist, the psychiatrist, and (possibly) a psychologist will evaluate you for suitability to transition. 

Whether you need a psychologist at CGH seems to be a subjective evaluation that depends on your age, to whether you have opted to socially transition, have done self-managed HRT, and presented as emotionally certain about dysphoria and transition, or have outside therapy and support. You can simply request for one if you'd like to have better support as well. 

The information we have on hand for NUH suggests that they do have a panel system like CGH, with a key difference being that they accept diagnoses from other doctors. You may be asked to see a psychiatrist for their evaluation, if you do not have a diagnosis or note from another doctor.

The first visit is where you may be asked to do a baseline blood test. Blood tests may include a liver function test, kidney function test, as well as hormone levels. You may be checked for growth hormone levels depending on your age. You can opt not to do so if you cannot afford it or ask for a more limited panel; blood tests will range from \$200 and should not be more than \$250 (for subsidised rates).

If you have already begun self-managing your own transition - via DIY medication - you can request for a blood test, though it will most likely be ordered for you.

Referrals to the speech therapist may take time, however, and may be less certain at NUH. (As NUH is in the same system as Alexandra Hospital \[AH\] you may be sent there instead.)

### Psychological medicine
{: #h-5657a66f-6ba7-4b79-8b43-5d873adb7950}

Psychological medicine - an umbrella term we are using for both psychology and psychiatry - is in an ideal world an unnecessary step in transition (with informed consent being the norm in many countries). Still, it is a necessary step in our medical system; you will need to be evaluated for gender dysphoria as defined in DSM-V and ICD-11 through the public health system if you are to be approved with HRT.

This has contributed, perhaps, to a certain degree of medicalisation surrounding transness. If you identify as something other than your birth gender, and the *transgender* label is something you’re comfortable with, no doctor should take that away from you. That said, our advice when you deal with a medical professional is to answer honestly; preparation will not be helpful, as some may be on the lookout for ‘drug-seeking behaviour’; some might also see cisness as the default and see preparation as evidence that one has been guided towards getting HRT. 

Inexperienced psychiatrists may quiz you on your non-binary gender identity if you choose to seek gender-affirming surgery later in life. Still, it's important for you to try and seek their approval and convince them that the surgery you're intending to get will make your life materially better — this will help with the hospital's evaluation of whether your surgery is 'cosmetic' or MediSave-eligible.

#### Having a first visit be at a psychiatrist/psychologist
{: #h-6f7abefa-52e0-4b4a-b516-5e4f5cf0d6da}

It is entirely possible that you may be sent to the Psychological Medicine department if you request to do so - for instance, if you have a separate issue that you wish to be looked at (depression, anxiety, ADHD or OCD, to list a few). Referrals to psychological medicine departments are often region-locked depending on where you live.

You can be referred to CGH Psychological Medicine department through Tampines, Bedok or Pasir Ris polyclinics, as that hospital has a smaller catchment area for their psychological medicine department.

As far as we know, a referral to NUH’s Psychological Medicine department is possible through any National University Polyclinic system.

If you do wish to ask for a referral to a psychological medicine department, you should also ask for a referral to the respective endocrinology departments during the polyclinic visit as well.

<u><strong>You should indicate that you are transgender when you seek a referral.</strong></u> If you don't, you may be assigned a psychiatrist that is not experienced with gender dysphoria. You may also be referred to someone who may not understand that they can refer you, who refuses to refer you, or someone who does not know that you can obtain HRT at the hospital. 

#### Psychiatry evaluation
{: #h-b45327ef-a756-440d-a211-27e197439a51}

At your first psychiatrist visit, you will be asked some basic questions regarding your gender dysphoria and your journey.

Questions you may be asked include - when you realised you are transgender, whether family or friends support you, what part of your body do you like or dislike, what type of company you would tend to be with, whether you have socially transitioned, as well as plans for your future in relation to transitioning.

Some other more stereotypical questions might also be asked - like what sort of toys you played with as a child, what clothes you like to wear, whether you’ve experienced abuse, whether you’ve had depression, if you have had previous health issues, or if you smoke or drink. As far as we know, these should not be determinants for the diagnosis you may be given.

In their evaluation, a psychiatrist is largely looking into whether:

1. you are capable of understanding what transition means for you
2. you are well enough or safe enough to transition (safety may be an issue on their minds)
3. you exhibit signs of gender dysphoria

Some may add a roadblock or two if you reveal that you have other issues - like anxiety or depression - and ask you to be assessed again. It is possible to have this relooked if you go to the endocrinologist.

At CGH, you will get an open-ended ticket with the Psychological Medicine department for two years. To keep the ticket open, you can opt to visit the psychiatrist or psychologist once every two years.

It is important that you obtain a memo from the psychiatrist once you are seeing one, either at NUH or CGH, if you are going for a medical evaluation for National Service-related issues.

#### Psychology evaluation
{: #h-e39ec670-c336-4829-ae66-7e81de0e2121}

At CGH, it’s likely that the primary objective for the psychologist visit would be for a panel assessment to evaluate if you are ready for HRT. Depending on which session comes first - the psychiatrist or the psychologist - the psychologist may refer to notes taken by the psychiatrist (and vice versa).

Like with the psychiatrist - they will seek a basic history of your life; information like your family members, relationships with them, and any compounding issues. The assessment here may not be psychiatric in nature; the psychologist may want to assess you on stressors that are external, like family issues and problems at work.

The psychologist sessions can be used as regular therapy sessions - if you feel you need psychological support for any issue, you can seek help from the psychologist.

CGH has also indicated that it may be offering family therapy - so this may be a good, low-cost way to work out any issues that you have with people who are close to you.

If you went through the CGH Endocrinology route, you will be given the option to see the psychologist.

At CGH, you will get an open-ended ticket with the Psychological Medicine department for two years. To keep the ticket open, you can simply visit the psychiatrist or psychologist once every two years.

### Private psychological assessments
{: #h-0b3252d9-4244-44aa-9d53-0377af45d82b}

A private psychological assessment through the private psychiatrists should be useful in acquiring HRT through most hospitals - with the only exception being CGH, which requires an in-house evaluation. Some private psychiatrists are affiliated with hospitals; ask if they have a particular recommendation and where they would refer you to in the public health system. (For instance, Dr Zheng is affiliated with TTSH.)

It is possible, at some gender-affirming GP clinics, to get HRT. An example of this would be Dr Pereira’s practice at [Pulse Clinic](https://www.pulse-clinic.com.sg/hormone-replacement-therapy-trans).
